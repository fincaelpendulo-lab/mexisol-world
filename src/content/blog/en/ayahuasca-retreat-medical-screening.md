Our ayahuasca retreat medical screening has three parts. We review every medication you take, and any taper is always planned with the doctor who prescribed it. We talk honestly about why you are coming. And we ask for your commitment to the process, before, during and after the week.

The first part keeps your body safe. The second and third are where the inner work begins. Many people expect a medical form and find a real conversation instead. This guide walks you through each part, what we ask, and why we sometimes say "not now."

At MexiSol World, nothing is confirmed until we have reviewed your health. Our retreats take place at Finca El Péndulo, near Tepatitlán, in Jalisco.

## Why ayahuasca retreat medical screening matters

Ayahuasca is a strong medicine, and it is not right for every body or every moment. The International Center for Ethnobotanical Education, Research and Service (ICEERS), a harm reduction group that has studied ayahuasca for years, puts it simply in its [AyaSafe resources](https://www.iceers.org/en/news/ayasafe-documents/): "Not everyone can or should take ayahuasca."

ICEERS also looked at the deaths linked to ayahuasca in media reports around the world. [Its 2023 report](https://www.iceers.org/ayahuasca-global-consumption-deaths/) counted 58. For scale, ICEERS estimates that about 820,000 people drank ayahuasca in 2019 alone. Not one autopsy blamed acute ayahuasca intoxication. The authors concluded that "the majority of deaths could have been prevented if the experiences had adhered to the minimum safety standards." These figures come from press reports, so they are rough. The lesson is not.

Screening is one of those minimum standards. As David Londoño, who helped create the AyaSafe documents, told ICEERS: "The better the screening, the greater the safety."

## Part one: your medications, reviewed with your own doctor

When you register, we ask about your medical history, every medication, supplement and herb you take, and any recent use of other substances. Then we go over it with you on a call.

Ayahuasca contains MAO inhibitors, which can react dangerously with many medicines. Antidepressants are the most common example, but the list also includes some painkillers, stimulants, sleeping pills, cough syrups and herbs like St. John's wort. Our [medical information page](../medical) has the full list of conditions and medications we ask about, and [which medications are unsafe with ayahuasca](../blog/medications-unsafe-with-ayahuasca) explains why.

What happens next depends on your answers:

- **Some conditions mean the retreat is not safe for you.** Epilepsy, a history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, and serious heart disease are examples.
- **Some only need clearance from your own doctor.**
- **Some mean you can join part of the week but not every medicine in it.**

If a medication has to stop, that decision belongs to you and the doctor who prescribed it. Never stop a prescribed medication on your own. We never give a taper plan or a timeline: your doctor does. Before you arrive, we confirm with you that the plan was followed and that you are safe to take part. If you take an antidepressant, [ayahuasca and SSRIs](../blog/ayahuasca-and-ssris) goes deeper.

### Why honesty here matters so much

Jerónimo Mazarrasa, Director of Social Innovation at ICEERS, told [Tripsitter in 2024](https://tripsitter.substack.com/p/responsible-plant-medicine-jeronimo-mazarrasa) that "safety begins with thorough screening." He also named the hard part. Some people, "out of desperation, may hide disqualifying factors like mental health issues or medications."

We understand that fear. If you have waited years for relief, a "not yet" can feel like a door closing. But we cannot keep you safe in ceremony if we do not know what is in your body. Tell us everything, even what seems minor.

## Part two: why you are coming

This is the part of our ayahuasca screening questions that surprises people most. We ask what brings you here, and we listen.

Some guests arrive with a clear wound: years of depression, grief, trauma or a pattern they cannot break. Others arrive with something harder to name. One guest wrote on [BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/organizers/o/ruger#reviews) in August 2026: "Before I came I felt very unsettled in my life and that I couldn’t find the answers I was seeking. I felt that I needed to try something new to provide clarity in my life." (Anonymous Yogi)

Both are honest reasons. What we listen for is not the "right" answer. We listen for whether you are coming for yourself, whether the timing is good, and whether what you hope for fits what a retreat week can hold.

### The reason under the reason

Often the first answer is not the whole one. Someone says they want to stop drinking. Underneath there may be loneliness, or a loss they never grieved. Gabor Maté, the physician who wrote *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts* (2010), says on [his website](https://drgabormate.com/addiction/) that "all addictions come from emotional loss, and exist to soothe the pain resulting from that loss."

Naming that pain out loud, sometimes for the first time, is already part of the work. It is also why a person in active dependence on alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids needs to be in a different place first. Arriving sober is required. A retreat is not rehab or detox, and we will tell you so plainly.

### Intention, not expectation

On [The Tim Ferriss Show in 2018](https://tim.blog/2018/02/20/gabor-mate/), Maté drew a line that we repeat to guests: the clear intention you set is not the same as an expectation. An intention points you in a direction. An expectation decides in advance what the medicine should give you.

Jenna Fletcher, writing for [Chacruna](https://chacruna.net/?p=1330), suggests spending the days before ceremony reflecting on why you feel drawn to ayahuasca, while accepting that what comes may not match what you asked for. Research points the same way. In a [2018 prospective study at Imperial College London](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2018.00897), 654 people planning to take a psychedelic answered surveys before and after. Those who arrived with clear intentions were more likely to report mystical-type experiences. The study was observational and self-selected, and it covered several psychedelics, not only ayahuasca. Still, it supports what many facilitators see.

## Part three: your commitment to the process

The last part of our screening is a question about you, not about your health: are you ready to do the work, before, during and after the week?

Before, that means following your doctor's plan, preparing your body and starting to look honestly at your life. During the week, it means showing up for each ceremony, each integration circle and the quiet hours in between. You are never pushed. You can choose a smaller dose and ask for more, and our team decides with you in the moment.

### Surrender

Commitment also means a willingness to let go. In his book *How to Change Your Mind* (2018), Michael Pollan wrote about his own guided sessions. Talking with [Tim Ferriss later that year](https://tim.blog/2018/06/26/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-michael-pollan/), he remembered his guides' "flight instructions": if you see anything scary, don't try to get away from it. In a hard moment, he told himself: "I just have to surrender to this."

The Imperial College study measured something similar. People who felt prepared, open and ready to surrender beforehand reported fewer challenging experiences. For us there are no good or bad ceremonies, only experiences. Tough moments are part of the process. Being willing to stay with them is part of the commitment.

### After the week

The week is a beginning. Pollan called the integration session after his experience "probably the most important part." Mazarrasa told Tripsitter that follow-up care, including integration, "is vital."

During the retreat there is group and one-on-one integration, and we stay in touch after you go home. But the daily choices that turn an insight into a changed habit are yours. Committing to that before you come makes the week more useful.

## What happens on the ayahuasca intake call

The intake call is relaxed. Think of it as a first conversation, not an exam.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| Before the call | You register and tell us your medical history, medications, supplements, herbs and any recent substance use. Have the list at hand. |
| On the call | We talk about what brings you, your health and medications, and which retreat and date make sense for you. You ask your own questions. |
| If something needs a change | You talk to your prescribing doctor. Your doctor sets any plan, and we review the timing with you. |
| Before you arrive | We confirm that any medical plan was followed and that you are safe to take part. |

Nothing is confirmed or charged until we have talked.

## When we say not now, and why

Sometimes the answer is "not yet." The medicine may not be safe with a condition you have. A medication may not be safe to stop. You may be in a crisis that needs stability first, or someone else may be pushing you to come.

Mazarrasa describes the skill of screening as "learning how to say no without pushing them toward less careful facilitators." That is how we try to say it. A "not now" from us comes with an explanation, and often with a different date or a next step, like talking to your doctor or giving yourself more time.

If you are still unsure about the timing, [is ayahuasca right for me?](../blog/is-ayahuasca-right-for-me) can help you think it through before the call.

## Frequently asked questions

**What questions are asked in ayahuasca screening?**
We ask about your medical history, every medication, supplement and herb you take, and any recent use of other substances. On the call we also ask what brings you and what you hope for, and whether you are ready to commit to preparation and integration.

**Who should not take ayahuasca?**
People with conditions such as epilepsy, a history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, serious heart disease, or a pregnancy, among others. People who take certain medications and cannot safely change them with their doctor. The full list is on our medical information page.

**Do I need to stop my medication before the retreat?**
Only your prescribing doctor can decide that. Some medicines are fine to keep taking, many are not, and some cannot be stopped safely. We review your list with you, and any change is planned with your doctor.

**Can I be honest about my mental health on the call?**
Please do. Knowing your history helps us keep you safe, and hiding it puts you at risk.

**Is the intake call a test I can fail?**
No. It is a conversation to see whether this retreat, at this moment, is safe and right for you. If it is not, we will tell you why and what could change.
