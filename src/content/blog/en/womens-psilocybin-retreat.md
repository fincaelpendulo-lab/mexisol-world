A women's psilocybin retreat is a week of mushroom ceremonies in a group of women, usually guided by women. The medicine is the same. What changes is the room: who sits beside you while you cry, move or lie still for hours, and who you talk to afterwards.

Women choose it for safety, for the ease of letting go among people who share their experience, and for the themes that tend to come up: the body, intuition, the roles they carry for everyone else. This guide explains why, what women describe, the long line of women healers behind this medicine, and what a held week looks like. Our retreats take place at Finca El Péndulo, near Tepatitlán, Jalisco.

## Why some women want a women's psilocybin retreat

Ceremony is vulnerable. On mushrooms you may weep, laugh, shake, need the bathroom, or lie curled on a mat for an hour. Many women tell us they want to know who will be in the room before they agree to that.

Part of this is about feeling safe enough to let go. In a circle of women, many find it easier to soften, to make noise, to be seen in a raw moment without thinking about how they look. In a women-led space, the people holding you and checking on you through the night know that feeling from the inside.

Part of it is about being heard. A [2025 scoping review in the International Journal of Drug Policy](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugpo.2025.104960) looked at 75 human studies of psychedelics for problems with alcohol or drugs. As its lead author, Alexandra Nigri, [wrote for Chacruna](https://chacruna.net/?p=130786), nine studies included no women at all and only 11 checked whether results differed for women. "Women were consistently underrepresented, rarely analyzed, and almost never discussed."

So when a woman wants to hear other women describe this experience, she is asking for something the field has not often given her.

## Psilocybin and intuition: what women describe

Many women who come to us use the same word: intuition. They feel they once knew what they wanted, and somewhere between work, family and caring for others, that inner voice went quiet.

The Jungian analyst Clarissa Pinkola Estés built a whole book around that loss. *[Women Who Run with the Wolves](https://penguinrandomhouse.com/books/47111/women-who-run-with-the-wolves-by-clarissa-pinkola-estes-phd)* (1992) begins from the idea that "within every woman there lives a powerful force, filled with good instincts, passionate creativity, and ageless knowing", and that it can be recovered.

Psilocybin does not hand you that voice. What people describe is closer to clearing the noise so they can hear it again.

The doctor Julia Mirer told the podcast Adventures of the Psyche how a psilocybin session she found almost uneventful changed the course of her life. It helped her let go of an inherited dream of practicing Western medicine. "I think what I really needed was to [connect back to my intuition](https://www.lucid.news/how-mushrooms-helped-me-doctor/)," she said. Insight does not always arrive with fireworks.

### From disconnection to connection

Research has words for this too. In a [2017 qualitative study in the Journal of Humanistic Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022167817709585), Rosalind Watts and colleagues at Imperial College London interviewed 20 people six months after a psilocybin trial for depression. Two changes stood out: a move from disconnection to connection, with themselves, others and the world, and a move from avoiding emotions to accepting them.

In her [TEDxOxford talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8kfGaVAXeMY), Watts described people going "from being emotionally locked up inside to being emotionally liberated." It is a small study with no control group, and the group was not only women. It still matches what many women tell us: the medicine opened a door to feelings they had stopped letting themselves have.

## The body in ceremony

Women often arrive living mostly from the neck up. They plan, manage and hold everyone else's needs in mind. In ceremony, attention drops into the body.

A [2017 study of 13 people](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022167817706884) who took psilocybin in a trial for cancer-related anxiety found "embodiment" in most of their interviews. Many also described a shift from separateness to interconnectedness, and some described surrender, or letting go. Moments of passing distress were common too. That is part of the work, not a sign that something went wrong. At MexiSol World we do not speak of good or bad experiences, only experiences.

Our psilocybin ceremonies are built to make room for the body. There is music, open space, and room to move or lie still. Around the ceremonies the week includes:

- **Temazcal,** the traditional Mexican sweat lodge, for heat, breath and release.
- **Voice activation,** for the women who have spent years keeping quiet.
- **Osho meditations,** with movement and stillness.
- **Time with the horses** in equine therapy.

## A lineage of women healers, from María Sabina to today

Mushroom ceremony in Mexico has a woman's face. María Sabina (1894 to 1985) was a Mazatec *sabia*, a wise woman, from Huautla, in the mountains of Oaxaca. She held *veladas*, night vigils with the mushrooms she called *niños santos*, the holy children.

Her story is not a simple one. The historian Osiris González Romero [writes in Chacruna](https://chacruna.net/maria-sabina-mushrooms-and-colonial-extractivism/) that she agreed to hold a ceremony for the American R. Gordon Wasson in 1955, under pressure from a local official. Wasson's article in Life magazine made her famous and helped spark the psychedelic counterculture of the 1960s. Afterwards her house was burned, her son was murdered, and she died in poverty.

She never took credit for the chants that made her famous. In a [2025 essay in The Brooklyn Rail](https://brooklynrail.org/2025/11/film/revisiting-nicolas-echevarria-maria-sabina-mujer-espiritu/), Maya Han writes that Sabina insisted she was "simply the 'interpreter'" through which the mushrooms spoke. In one of her chants she calls herself "the woman who looks inward."

Han's essay looks at Nicolás Echevarría's documentary *María Sabina, mujer espíritu*. She notes that the ceremony it films "foregrounds the females, rendering the event in effect a women's space", and that it shows "the sisterhood that binds la sabia with her female relatives, friends, and neighbors." A women's circle around the mushrooms is not a new idea. It is close to how the ceremony looked in Huautla.

### More than one name

Sabina's life in her own words is in Álvaro Estrada's *[Vida de María Sabina](https://www.caniem.org/catalogo-venta-derechos/en/product/vida-de-maria-sabina-la-sabia-de-los-hongos/)* (Siglo XXI, 1977), an oral autobiography he recorded and translated from Mazatec. The Chacruna anthology *[Women & Psychedelics: Uncovering Invisible Voices](https://chacruna.net/women-psychedelics-invisible-voices/)* (2022 in Spanish, 2024 in English) widens the picture. It profiles the Shipibo healer Wilma Mahua Campos and Amada Cardenas, who opened her Texas home to people seeking peyote, among many others.

We are not Mazatec, and we do not claim their tradition. Our mushrooms are sourced in Mexico, and Krista, who leads our psilocybin ceremonies, learned to work with them in Oaxaca. We hold that history with respect.

## What a held week looks like, beyond the ceremony

A retreat is not two ceremonies with some waiting in between. Most of the change happens in the days around them: in what you notice, say out loud and decide to keep.

Our psilocybin retreat is seven days and six nights, with up to 15 guests. It includes two psilocybin ceremonies and one with Satori, hapé and cacao ceremonies, voice activation, equine therapy, Osho meditations, temazcal, a fire circle, a sunrise hike, all meals, and group and one-on-one integration. The full plan is on the [psilocybin retreat](../psilocybin-retreat) page.

Your dose is decided in conversation with Krista before each ceremony, and again in the moment. You can choose a smaller dose and ask for more medicine later. Many women who come from microdosing at home like knowing they can start gently.

Being held is not the same as being steered. Watts said of her trial that the therapist "doesn't structure the sessions at all or direct the content in any way." Mary Cosimano, of the Johns Hopkins psilocybin research team, put the other half simply in her [TEDxKC talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/mary_cosimano_psilocybin_love_and_the_meaning_of_life): "love is literally part of the answer." Our job is to keep you safe and cared for so the medicine can do its part.

## Who holds the space with you

Krista was born in Mexico City and trained as an engineer. She was a high school principal for 20 years before she came to the Finca as a volunteer and stayed. Her ceremonies are calm, practical and warm.

Around her, most of the team are women, and our integration coach is a woman. She works with you in the days after the ceremonies, in the group and one-on-one.

Medical screening is required before any retreat. We review your medications, talk about why you are coming, and ask for your commitment to the process. Any change to a prescribed medication is made only with the doctor who prescribed it. A personal history of psychosis, schizophrenia or bipolar disorder is a reason not to attend, and because the week includes Satori, the MAO-inhibitor medication list applies too. See our [medical information](../medical). You must also arrive sober.

A retreat is not therapy, and it is not a treatment. It is a space for inner work, held with care.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a women's psilocybin retreat safer than a mixed one?**
Not automatically. Safety comes from careful screening, clear agreements shared before you arrive, experienced guides and real aftercare. Ask any retreat how it handles all four, whoever is in the group.

**I have only microdosed at home. Is a ceremony too much?**
Many of our guests come from microdosing. A full ceremony is a different experience, which is why the dose is agreed with Krista and you can start smaller and ask for more. If you are weighing mushrooms against ayahuasca, read [ayahuasca or psilocybin](../blog/ayahuasca-or-psilocybin).

**What if I take prescribed medication?**
Tell us during screening. Some medications do not mix with psilocybin or Satori. Any change is planned only with the doctor who prescribed it, never on your own.

**Is the November women's retreat in English?**
No, it is held in Spanish. If that does not work for you, book a call and we will go over our other psilocybin dates together.
