How to set an intention for ayahuasca comes down to two steps: ask yourself honestly why you are coming, then turn the answer into one short, personal sentence about what you want to understand or meet, not what you want to get. Bring it to the ceremony, say it once, and then let it go. The medicine often answers a different question from the one you asked, and that is part of the work.

This guide is for people who keep hearing "you need an intention" and are not sure what a good one sounds like. You may have carried anxiety, grief or an old wound for years, and you want this to count. Here is how to find yours and what to do with it.

## What an intention is (and is not)

An intention is a direction. It tells you, and the people holding the space, where your heart is pointing. It is not an order for the medicine, and it is not a treatment plan.

The Chacruna Institute puts it simply in [7 Ways to Cultivate Your Ayahuasca Experience](https://chacruna.net/?p=1330): "Some individuals set an intention to define what it is they plan to learn or resolve through the experience." Learn or resolve. Not acquire.

Tim Ferriss made the key distinction in his [2018 conversation with the physician Gabor Maté](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H9B5mYfBPlY): "you set a clear intention, but the clear intention is not the same as an expectation."

| | Intention | Expectation | Demand |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sounds like | "Show me what I need to see about my anger" | "I will finally see my childhood" | "Take my depression away" |
| Where it points | Toward understanding | Toward a picture you already have | Toward a result |
| When the night goes elsewhere | You stay curious | You feel let down | You fight the experience |

The scholar Jeffrey Breau, writing for the [Harvard Study of Psychedelics in Society and Culture](https://psychedelics-study.harvard.edu/?p=2067), sees intention-setting as a ritual that marks the experience "as special and sacred." Part of what an intention does is tell you that this night is not ordinary.

## Why intention matters: what research and teachers say

There is some evidence that intention shapes the experience. In a [2018 prospective study in Frontiers in Pharmacology](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2018.00897), a team that included Robin Carhart-Harris and David Nutt followed 654 people who were planning a psychedelic experience on their own. They surveyed them before and up to four weeks after.

Two findings stand out:

- **Clear intentions** went with more mystical-type experiences, the kind people often describe as deeply meaningful.
- **Feeling prepared, open and ready to surrender**, with low anxiety, went with fewer challenging experiences.

The study was observational, the participants chose themselves, and many dropped out by four weeks (212 remained). So it shows a link, not proof. Still, it matches what experienced guides have long said: be clear, and be willing to let go.

Michael Pollan, who wrote [How to Change Your Mind](https://michaelpollan.com/books/how-to-change-your-mind/) (2018), told the [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2018/06/michael-pollan-takes-a-trip/) that letting go depends on trust: "You're only ever going to allow that to happen if you feel safe." This is why the setting and the people around you matter as much as the words you choose.

## How to set an intention for ayahuasca: questions to find yours

Your intention usually already exists. It is hidden inside your reason for coming. That is why our first conversation with every guest, and part of our medical screening, is about why you want to sit with the medicine at all. Often the intention grows straight out of that talk.

Take an hour, a notebook and some quiet. Answer these without editing:

1. **What made me start looking for this?** Name the moment, not the diagnosis.
2. **What do I keep carrying that I am tired of carrying?** A feeling, a memory, a relationship, a habit.
3. **What am I afraid I will see?** Fear often points to exactly where the work is.
4. **Who do I want to be with the people I love?**
5. **If this week went well, what would be different in how I live, not in how I feel for a day?**

Then look for the thread. Most people find one sentence keeps returning. Write it in the first person, in the present, and keep it short enough to remember with your eyes closed.

The psychologist Rachel Harris has written about ayahuasca for years. Her book [Listening to Ayahuasca](https://newworldlibrary.com/product/listening-to-ayahuasca) (2017) is a good companion if you want to go deeper; her publisher describes it as "helping readers clarify their intentions."

### A few simple rules

- **One intention, not a list.** If you have five, ask which one sits underneath the others.
- **About you, not about others.** "I want to understand my part in my marriage", not "I want my husband to change".
- **Ask to understand, not to be fixed.** The medicine is not a treatment, and an intention is not a treatment goal.
- **Make it honest, not impressive.** "I want to stop feeling numb" is a beautiful intention.

## Ayahuasca intention examples: from vague to clear

Vague intentions are not wrong. They are simply hard to hold. Here is how a few common ones can become clearer, without becoming demands.

| Vague | Demanding | Clear and open |
|---|---|---|
| "I want to heal" | "Heal my depression tonight" | "Show me what I need to understand about my sadness" |
| "I want answers" | "Tell me if I should leave my job" | "Help me hear what I already know about my work" |
| "I want to be happy" | "Make me feel joy again" | "I want to see what keeps me from feeling joy" |
| "I want to let go of the past" | "Erase what my father did" | "I am ready to meet the anger I carry about my father" |
| "I want to love myself" | "Stop my self-hatred" | "Show me how I speak to myself, and what it costs me" |

Gabor Maté described his own version on the same podcast: "the specific intention is what do I want to learn tonight." He added that the more specific it is to where you are in life, "the more effective it's going to be."

## Hold it lightly: surrender in ceremony

This is the paradox: you work hard to find a clear intention, and then you let go of it.

In our ayahuasca retreat the opening day begins with intentions, shared with Humberto, who leads the ceremonies. That night, you bring your intention to the circle, say it inwardly when you drink, and then let the night be what it is. Our [preparation page](../prepare) says it plainly: come with a loose intention, or none at all, because the medicine rarely follows a plan.

The Chacruna article gives the same advice: "release your expectations of what you think will happen." And: "You will get what you need, not what you want."

Maté warned Ferriss about the alternative: "some people sit through the whole night resisting their experience because it doesn't meet their expectations." Pollan described what his guides taught him instead: "you have to move toward even the things that seem scary, and not to resist."

### What this looks like in practice

- **Say it once, then rest.** You do not need to repeat it all night. Return to it the way you return to the breath in meditation, when you notice you are lost.
- **Use it as an anchor, not a map.** If the night gets hard, your intention can remind you why you came.
- **Follow what comes, even if it seems unrelated.** A memory, a sensation, a song. Stay with it.
- **Ask for help when you need it.** Humberto, the musicians and the servers are with you through the night.

At MexiSol World we say there are no good or bad experiences, only experiences. A quiet night, a confusing night and a hard night can all be answering your intention. If this is your first time, our guide to [what happens in an ayahuasca ceremony](../blog/first-ayahuasca-ceremony) walks you through the night.

## When the medicine answers another question

Many people come out of ceremony and say: "That was not what I asked about." Often, days later, they add: "But it was what I needed."

Dennis McKenna, an ethnopharmacologist who has sat with ayahuasca hundreds of times, told [Tim Ferriss in 2021](https://tim.blog/2021/07/25/dennis-mckenna-transcript/): "There is no standard message." Maté put it this way: "it's not that the ayahuasca comes with an agenda. It works through you." And: "whatever experience you have, that's the experience you need to have."

Research hints at this too. In a [2021 qualitative study in the Journal of Humanistic Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1177/00221678211008331), researchers interviewed 41 Westerners about their intentions for drinking ayahuasca and the impact they felt afterwards. Besides changes in mood, habits and relationships, people described effects they had not been looking for: in creativity, their bodies, their connection to nature, their spirituality and their concern for others. It is a small interview study, so it tells us what people report, not what will happen to you.

Itzhak Beery, a shamanic practitioner and author, wrote in [Lucid News](https://www.lucid.news/?p=2069) about a healer who, when people said they had seen nothing, simply asked: "What did you feel?" Sometimes the answer to your intention is a feeling, not a vision.

One of our own guests wrote in a [public review on BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/r/7-day-ayahuasca-retreat-at-an-authentic-finca-in-mexico-69537), signed "Anonymous Yogi": "Before I came I felt very unsettled in my life and that I couldn’t find the answers I was seeking." What this guest described afterwards was not a single answer but a change in how she lives: "I now pay more attention to listening to what my body is telling me, and I became willing to journal my feelings."

Our article on [what ayahuasca teaches](../blog/what-ayahuasca-teaches) collects the lessons people most often bring home.

## Coming back to your intention after the retreat

An intention does not end when the ceremony does. The real question is whether it changes how you live on an ordinary Tuesday.

During the retreat we hold group integration with Humberto and one-on-one time, and the week closes with a temazcal and a cacao ceremony in the Osho Hut. That is where many guests first see how the night answered their intention, sometimes sideways.

At home, try this:

1. **Reread what you wrote before the retreat.** Then write what actually happened, without judging it.
2. **Look for the link.** Ask: if the medicine was answering my intention, what was it saying?
3. **Rewrite your intention as a practice.** "I want to see what keeps me from joy" can become "Every morning, I notice one thing I enjoy."
4. **Choose one small action for this week.** One conversation, one boundary, one habit.
5. **Come back to it each month.** Intentions change as you do. Let yours grow.

McKenna's advice fits here: "Keep listening." Our guide to [the days after ayahuasca](../blog/after-ayahuasca-integration) covers the first weeks at home in more detail.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should my intention be for ayahuasca?**
Something honest and personal about what you want to understand, meet or let go of. Good intentions are short, about you, and open: "Show me what I need to see about my fear" rather than "Take my fear away." If you are not sure, start with why you want to come at all.

**What if I don't have an intention?**
That is fine. Some people come with only a feeling or a question, and some come with none. Our preparation advice is to come with a loose intention, or none at all. Openness and willingness to surrender matter more than the right words.

**Should I write my intention down?**
Yes. Writing helps you find the one sentence underneath many thoughts, and gives you something to reread after the retreat.

**Can I change my intention between ceremonies?**
Yes. Many people adjust their intention after the first night, based on what came up. Others keep the same one all week. Talk it through with Humberto in the morning if you are unsure.

**What if the ceremony had nothing to do with my intention?**
This is common, and it does not mean it failed. The connection often shows up days or weeks later.
