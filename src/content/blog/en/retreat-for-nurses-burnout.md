A retreat for nurses, burnout and all, is a week where, for once, someone else holds the space. You do not chart, triage or watch the monitors. You rest, sit in ceremony, and are looked after by a team whose only job that week is you.

It is not a treatment for burnout, and we will not pretend it is. What it can offer is room: to feel what years of caring have pushed down, to remember why you chose this work, and to practice receiving. This guide is for nurses, therapists, counselors and anyone who gives care all day and goes home empty. Our retreats take place at Finca El Péndulo, near Tepatitlán, Jalisco.

## Compassion fatigue: when caring empties you

Most caregivers do not burn out from one bad shift. It builds slowly, from years of standing close to other people's pain.

Laura van Dernoot Lipsky has spent her career with trauma workers, and once worked at Harborview, the level one trauma center for the US Northwest. In her TEDx talk [Beyond the Cliff](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uOzDGrcvmus), she tells of standing on a cliff on a family holiday in the Caribbean. Her first thought was how beautiful it was. Her second was where the rescue helicopter would land. Her family went quiet, until her stepfather-in-law asked: "Are you sure all this trauma work hasn't gotten to you?"

She says the toll "is slow moving." It is hard to see from the inside, and it isolates you. People call it compassion fatigue, vicarious trauma or secondary trauma. In her book *[Trauma Stewardship](https://traumastewardship.com/trauma-stewardship-book/)* (2009, with Connie Burk) she lists 16 warning signs. In the talk she names some of them:

- Feeling you are never doing enough
- Hypervigilance, never fully off duty
- An exhaustion where "you are tired in your soul"
- Avoidance, and cynical humor
- Anger, addictions and numbing

Numbing is the one she warns about most. "You don't get to selectively numb," she says. If you numb the sorrow, you also numb the joy.

Bedside nurses know this from the inside. One participant in a recent study of frontline clinicians put it plainly, as the lead investigator [recalled to UW Medicine](https://newsroom.uw.edu/news-releases/psilocybin-therapy-helps-clinicians-process-covid-despair): "I feel like a robot." They knew they were saying the right things to families. They felt nothing.

## Why caregivers find it hard to receive

If you are a nurse, you probably know how to care for everyone except yourself. That is not a personal flaw. It is often how the role is learned.

In a [2020 study in the International Journal of Nursing Studies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijnurstu.2019.103436), Hannah Andrews and colleagues interviewed 30 nurses in the UK about self-care. The core finding was that nurses felt they needed permission, from others and from themselves, to care for themselves. Self-compassion was what they struggled with most.

The psychologist Kristin Neff has studied self-compassion for years. In her [TEDx talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IvtZBUSplr4) she says: "We say things to ourselves we would never say to someone we cared about." She describes self-compassion as treating yourself as you would a good friend, remembering that struggle is part of being human, and noticing your own pain instead of rushing past it.

She also answers the fear many caregivers have, that caring for yourself is selfish. "The more we were able to keep our hearts open to ourselves, the more we have available to give to others."

### Being cared for is a practice too

A week away will not undo years of habit. But for many caregivers it is the first time in a long while that someone else plans the meals, checks on them at night and asks how they are, and means it.

Some find that harder than they expected. Letting someone bring you water, or sit with you while you cry, can feel strange when you are always the one who brings the water. That discomfort is part of the work.

## Reconnecting with why you chose this work

Most nurses and therapists did not choose the work for the pay. Andrews' study found that for many, nursing felt like a calling rather than a job. Burnout can bury that calling under paperwork, staffing gaps and grief.

The physician Rachel Naomi Remen spent more than twenty years counseling people with chronic and terminal illness. Her book *[Kitchen Table Wisdom](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/348294/kitchen-table-wisdom-by-rachel-naomi-remen/)* (1996) is a collection of stories from that work. In an essay for Lion's Roar, [Helping, Fixing or Serving?](https://www.lionsroar.com/helping-fixing-or-serving/), she draws a line many caregivers recognize:

> "When you help, you see life as weak. When you fix, you see life as broken. When you serve, you see life as whole."

"Fixing and helping are draining, and over time we may burn out," she writes, "but service is renewing." Service, in her words, is "a relationship between equals."

Ram Dass and Paul Gorman made a similar case in *[How Can I Help?](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/a/ram-dass-paul-gorman/how-can-i-help1/)* (1985), a book written largely for nurses, doctors and social workers. Their idea is that your inner work and your work for others feed each other. Not every reader agrees: the Kirkus review found it left too little room for tragedy. Still, the question at its center is a useful one to bring into ceremony.

Lipsky ends her talk with a line from Howard Thurman: "what the world needs is people who have come alive." Many caregivers want exactly that: not a new job, but to feel alive in the one they have.

## What research on clinicians found, and its limits

There is one randomized trial that speaks directly to healthcare workers. It is small, and it is worth reading carefully.

In a [2024 trial in JAMA Network Open](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.49026), Anthony Back and colleagues at the University of Washington enrolled 30 physicians, advanced practice clinicians and nurses. All had worked on the front line during COVID-19, had no mental health diagnosis before the pandemic, and had moderate or severe symptoms of depression. Half received one psilocybin session, half a niacin placebo. Both groups had two preparation visits and three integration visits.

| Outcome at day 28 | Psilocybin | Placebo | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depression (MADRS) | 21.33 points lower | 9.33 points lower | Significant difference |
| Burnout (Stanford Professional Fulfillment Index) | 6.40 points lower | 2.33 points lower | Not statistically significant |
| PTSD symptoms | Larger drop | Smaller drop | Not formally tested |

So the trial found a clear effect on depression. On burnout, the change leaned the same way but did not reach significance. As [Filter reported](https://filtermag.org/psilocybin-doctors-nurses-covid/), the sample was small, many participants could tell which group they were in, and the placebo group was offered psilocybin after day 28. This was clinical therapy in a research setting, not a retreat.

The words of the participants say more than the numbers. A critical care flight nurse, Will Koenig, said placebo counseling brought "no change, no improvement, no decrease in burnout," and his psilocybin session was "completely different." "It gave me this sense that (human) suffering can be transcended and transformed, and that's our natural state," [he told UW Medicine](https://newsroom.uw.edu/news-releases/psilocybin-therapy-helps-clinicians-process-covid-despair). Other participants, Filter reported, said "I was shown my tendency to be a superhero," and "I don't have to carry it all by myself."

If you are curious about why insight can feel so fresh after a ceremony, our post on [psilocybin and neuroplasticity](../blog/psilocybin-neuroplasticity) covers what the research says, and what it does not.

## A retreat for nurses with burnout: what the week looks like

Our retreats are seven days and six nights, with up to 15 guests. There are two formats. The ayahuasca retreat, with Humberto, has two ayahuasca ceremonies and one Satori ceremony. The psilocybin retreat, with Krista, has two mushroom ceremonies and one with Satori. If you are unsure which suits you, read [is ayahuasca right for me?](../blog/is-ayahuasca-right-for-me)

Around the ceremonies, the week is built for rest and for feeling:

- **All meals and six nights,** so you do not plan or cook anything
- **Temazcal and a fire circle,** for heat, breath and letting go
- **Cacao and hapé ceremonies,** plus voice activation and time with the horses on the psilocybin week
- **Group and one-on-one integration,** to put what came up into words

When you have spent years looking after others, it matters who will look after you. Most of our team are women, and our integration coach is a woman. Your dose is decided in conversation, and you can choose a smaller dose and ask for more medicine later.

At MexiSol World we do not talk about good or bad experiences, only experiences. A hard night, full of grief you did not know you were carrying, is part of the process. You do not have to manage it alone or manage anyone else's.

Guests often describe this in simple words. "From the first day, I felt cared for and safe," Jay Poindexter [wrote on BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/organizers/o/ruger#reviews). Another guest, shown there as Anonymous Yogi, wrote that "the staff, all of them, provided the love, the insight, and most of all, the physical and mental support that I needed."

### Screening comes first

Medical screening is required before any retreat. We review your medications, talk about why you are coming, and ask for your commitment to the process. Any change to a prescribed medication is made only with the doctor who prescribed it. Some conditions, such as a personal history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, are a reason not to attend. Read our [medical information](../medical) before you book. You must arrive sober.

A retreat is not therapy, rehab or a treatment for burnout or depression. Questions about your license or your employer's rules are yours to check with them; we cannot advise on them.

## Bringing it back to your shifts

The week ends. The unit, the caseload and the night shifts are still there. What changes, if anything, is how you meet them.

Integration is the slow part. Insights from ceremony fade if they are not turned into small, concrete choices. Some ideas from the people quoted here:

1. **Notice the signs early.** Keep Lipsky's list somewhere you can see it. Numbing and cynical humor are signals, not personality.
2. **Speak to yourself as a friend would.** Neff's practice is simple and can be done in a hospital bathroom: name that this is hard, and that you are not alone in it.
3. **Ask for help before you need it.** If permission is the problem, as Andrews found, practice giving it to yourself in small ways.
4. **Return to service, not rescue.** Remen's distinction can change how a shift feels, even when nothing about the shift changes.
5. **Keep talking.** Integration continues after you leave. Our post on [what happens after ayahuasca](../blog/after-ayahuasca-integration) describes the first weeks home.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a plant medicine retreat cure burnout?**
No, and we do not offer it as a cure. The one randomized trial with clinicians found a significant drop in depression, but its burnout result was not statistically significant. What we offer is a week of rest, ceremony and integration, with screening and support around it.

**Is this a retreat only for nurses and healthcare workers?**
No. Our groups are mixed and open to anyone who passes screening. Nurses, therapists, counselors and people caring for a sick family member are all welcome, and you may find others in the circle who carry a similar weight.

**I take an antidepressant. Can I still come?**
Tell us during screening. Several antidepressants do not mix with ayahuasca, psilocybin or Satori. Any change is planned only with your prescribing doctor, never on your own.

**How do I take a week off when my unit is short-staffed?**
That is a real barrier, and only you can weigh it. We publish our dates well ahead so you can request leave early. Our next retreats are listed on the [upcoming retreats](../upcoming-retreats) page.
