Can ayahuasca help with addiction? Some people say a ceremony showed them the pain behind their drinking or using, and that this was the start of real change. Small studies point the same way, but they are early, and no one can promise that a ceremony will end a habit.

What we can say is this. Ayahuasca for addiction is not a pill or a detox. It is an inner experience that can show you why you reach for the bottle, the line or the screen. The change itself happens in the months after, in how you live.

This article is for anyone who has been fighting a pattern for years, or who loves someone who is. We look at the spiritual view of addiction, at what people who broke a cycle describe, at what the research found and where it stops, and at the plain rules we keep at MexiSol World.

## Addiction as a thirst: Jung, Bill W. and spiritus contra spiritum

In January 1961, Carl Jung answered a letter from Bill Wilson, the co-founder of Alcoholics Anonymous. They were writing about Roland H., a former patient of Jung's.

Jung wrote that his patient's "craving for alcohol was the equivalent on a low level of the spiritual thirst of our being for wholeness." The [letter, dated 30 January 1961](https://wccm.org/outreach-areas/addiction-recovery/carl-jung-letter-to-bill-w), is collected in [C.G. Jung Letters, Volume 2](https://press.princeton.edu/isbn/9780691097244).

He then pointed to the Latin word for alcohol, *spiritus*, the same word used for spirit. "The helpful formula therefore is: spiritus contra spiritum." Spirit against spirit.

Jung did not say how that thirst gets met. He named three roads: an act of grace, honest contact with friends, or a "higher education of the mind." Many people who come to ceremony are looking for the first, and find they also need the second.

That idea is the thread of this whole article. If the habit is a thirst for something deeper, then stopping the habit is only half the work. The other half is finding what you were thirsty for.

## Gabor Maté: not why the addiction, but why the pain

No one has made this view better known than Gabor Maté. He worked for 13 years as a physician in Vancouver's Downtown Eastside, as the [Georgia Straight reported](https://www.straight.com/node/918656), with people living with severe addiction.

In his book [In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts](https://www.northatlanticbooks.com/shop/in-the-realm-of-hungry-ghosts/), he traces the roots of addiction to childhood trauma and argues for self-compassion in recovery. The title borrows the image of hungry ghosts, which Maté describes as creatures with "large empty bellies and small, scrawny necks and tiny little mouths." They can never be filled.

In his [TEDxRio+20 talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=66cYcSak6nE), Maté defines addiction as "any behavior that gives you temporary relief, temporary pleasure, but in the long term, causes harm," and that you can't give up despite the harm. Then he turns the usual question around: "the real question in addiction is not why the addiction, but why the pain?"

For someone who has tried to quit many times, this matters. Willpower aimed at the habit often fails because the habit is doing a job. It is numbing something. Ceremony, at its best, lets you look at that something.

### What Maté says about ayahuasca

Maté sat with ayahuasca for the first time in 2008. He told the Georgia Straight that it "put me in touch with a deep love that I both wanted and had been running away from all my life."

In a [long interview on The Tim Ferriss Show](https://tim.blog/2018/02/20/gabor-mate/), he described crying "tears of joy." He was also honest: "I wish I could say that, after that experience, I became a loving husband and a loving human being." One night opened a door. Walking through it took years.

He said he has seen people work through addictions with the medicine "so long as the proper integration is done afterwards." And he warned that it should be done with practitioners of deep integrity, that some healers exploit people, and that psychedelics "are not for everybody."

## Ayahuasca addiction stories: what people describe

The people who speak about this most clearly rarely say the medicine took the craving away. They say it showed them something.

Deborah Parrish Snyder, interviewed by [Lucid News](https://www.lucid.news/?p=4641), describes how ayahuasca brought her dependence on alcohol into view. She had struggled with the idea of a higher power outside herself. In ceremony she found something else: "Frankly, the power that was greater than me was inside of myself, in that voice that emerged in my psyche."

In [a 2019 qualitative study](https://doi.org/10.1111/dar.12985), researchers interviewed 11 Indigenous participants in Canada six months after ayahuasca retreats for addiction and stress. All 11 described using less and craving less. Eight said they had stopped at least one substance.

What they credited most was not the visions. It was a new sense of connection: with themselves, with other people and with nature or spirit. The authors called it "a key element associated with reduced substance use and cravings." Jung would have recognized it.

Change often looks smaller and quieter than people expect. One guest on our [BookRetreats page](https://bookretreats.com/r/7-day-ayahuasca-retreat-at-an-authentic-finca-in-mexico-69537), Anonymous Yogi, wrote: "I now pay more attention to listening to what my body is telling me, and I became willing to journal my feelings." New habits start like that.

## Ayahuasca for addiction: what the research found

The research on ayahuasca for addiction is real but small. Here is what the main studies found, in plain terms.

| Study | Who | What it found | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Thomas and colleagues, 2013](https://doi.org/10.2174/15733998113099990003), Current Drug Abuse Reviews | 12 people in a First Nations community in British Columbia; four days of group counselling with two ayahuasca ceremonies | After six months: more hope, empowerment, mindfulness and quality of life. Alcohol, tobacco and cocaine use fell; only the drop in cocaine was statistically significant. Cannabis and opiate use did not change | Very small, no control group |
| [Loizaga-Velder and Verres, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/02791072.2013.873157), Journal of Psychoactive Drugs | Interviews with 13 therapists, 2 researchers and 14 people treated with ayahuasca for addiction in South America | In carefully structured settings, ayahuasca may support recovery and help prevent relapse. Results depend on many factors | Qualitative, self-report |
| [Fábregas and colleagues, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2010.03.024), Drug and Alcohol Dependence | 127 ritual ayahuasca users in Brazil and 115 people who did not drink it | Ritual users scored lower on alcohol use and psychiatric problems on a standard addiction scale | Shows an association, not a cause |

There is also a bigger trial with a related medicine. In a [2022 randomized trial in JAMA Psychiatry](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2022.2096), 95 adults with alcohol use disorder received psilocybin or an active placebo, plus 12 weeks of psychotherapy. Over 32 weeks, heavy drinking days were 9.7% in the psilocybin group and 23.6% in the placebo group.

That trial was psilocybin, not ayahuasca, and it came with months of therapy. Still, it shows the same shape: the medicine opens something, and the steady work around it carries the change.

So the honest summary is this. People report real shifts. Early studies agree. Large controlled trials of ayahuasca for addiction have not been done yet.

## Ayahuasca vs ibogaine

People searching for plant medicine for alcoholism or opioids often find ibogaine too. They are very different.

According to a [2026 review in the journal Addiction](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.70319), early studies suggest ibogaine can reduce craving and withdrawal in people dependent on opioids or cocaine. The same review warns that it can disturb the heart's rhythm, with potentially fatal arrhythmias reported even in people with no known heart condition.

We do not offer ibogaine. And ayahuasca is not a withdrawal treatment. If you need help with withdrawal, that is medical care, and it comes before any retreat.

## Why you must arrive sober, and why we are not rehab

This is the part we say most plainly. A retreat with MexiSol World is not rehab, detox or therapy. You need to arrive free of alcohol and drugs.

There are two reasons. The first is safety. Ayahuasca contains MAO inhibitors that react with many substances and medicines, and active dependence on alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids is on our list of conditions where the retreat may not be safe. You can read the full list on our [medical information page](../medical).

The second reason is the work itself. A ceremony asks you to be present with what comes up. That is hard to do while your body is still in withdrawal or still wanting the next drink.

### How screening works

Medical screening is required before every retreat. Nothing is confirmed until we have talked. With you, we look at three things:

1. **Your medications.** We review everything you take. If something needs to change, that happens only with the doctor who prescribed it. Never stop a prescribed medication on your own.
2. **Why you are coming.** We want to understand what you hope for and where you are with the pattern you want to change.
3. **Your commitment.** The retreat is one week. The process of change is longer, and we ask whether you are ready for it.

If you are not yet at a point where you can stop on your own, we will tell you honestly, and the right first step is medical or addiction care. Our guide on [medications that are unsafe with ayahuasca](../blog/medications-unsafe-with-ayahuasca) goes deeper, and [is ayahuasca right for me?](../blog/is-ayahuasca-right-for-me) can help you decide.

## The real work starts after the ceremony

The week itself is short. Our ayahuasca retreat takes place at Finca El Péndulo, near Tepatitlán in Jalisco. Over seven days there are two ayahuasca ceremonies and one Satori ceremony, led by Humberto, with group and one-on-one integration, a temazcal and a fire circle.

A ceremony can show you the pain. It can give you a taste of what Jung called wholeness. But a habit lives in your days: in the hour after work, in the friends who drink, in the stress you have no other way to release.

James Clear puts it bluntly in [Atomic Habits](https://jamesclear.com/atomic-habits): "You do not rise to the level of your goals. You fall to the level of your systems." Insight is not a system. What you build after is.

Some things that help people turn an insight into a new pattern:

- **Write down what you saw, the morning after.** Keep it short and in your own words.
- **Pick one change.** One habit you will drop, and one thing you will put in its place.
- **Find your people.** Jung's second road was honest contact with friends. A recovery group, a counselor, an integration circle or a sober friend.
- **Plan the hard hours.** Know what you will do at the moment the old habit usually wins.
- **Keep talking to professionals.** If you are in addiction treatment or therapy, keep going. A retreat is not a replacement.

Many people find that integration takes two to three months. We stay in touch after you go home. Our article on [the days after ayahuasca](../blog/after-ayahuasca-integration) covers that time in detail.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can ayahuasca cure addiction?**
No. Nothing we offer cures addiction, and we would never promise sobriety. Some people describe a ceremony as the moment they understood their pattern, and small studies report less use afterwards, but the change depends on the work you do after.

**Can I come to an ayahuasca retreat to stop drinking?**
Only if you can already stop before you arrive. You must come free of alcohol and drugs, and active dependence on alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids is a reason the retreat may not be safe. If you need help with withdrawal, start with medical care.

**What did Jung mean by spiritus contra spiritum?**
In his 1961 letter to Bill W., Jung said the craving for alcohol was a low form of the spiritual thirst for wholeness. *Spiritus* is Latin for both alcohol and spirit. His formula, spirit against spirit, means the thirst is best met by a real spiritual experience and honest human connection.

**Is ayahuasca better than ibogaine for addiction?**
They are different medicines with different risks. Ibogaine has been studied mainly for opioid and cocaine withdrawal and carries a known risk to the heart. We do not offer ibogaine, and ayahuasca is not a withdrawal treatment.
