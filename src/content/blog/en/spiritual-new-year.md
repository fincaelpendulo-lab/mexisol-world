A spiritual way to celebrate New Year has two movements: first you close the year that is ending, then you open the next one with an intention. Closing means naming what you are ready to let go of, often with a simple ritual of fire, writing or water. Opening means choosing who you want to be, not only what you want to get.

This guide is for anyone who has had a hard or strange year and does not want another loud party or a list of resolutions that is gone by February. We look at why intentions tend to outlast resolutions, how release rituals work, what Mexican tradition has to teach about ending a cycle, and a ritual you can do at home on December 31.

## Why resolutions fade and intentions last

Resolutions are not useless. In a [2002 study in the Journal of Clinical Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.1151), John Norcross and colleagues followed 159 people who made New Year's resolutions and 123 who wanted to change but made none. At six months, 46% of the resolvers were still succeeding, against 4% of the others.

A [2020 trial in PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0234097) with 1,066 Swedish adults found something more useful. After a year, 55% considered themselves successful. People who framed their goal as something to move toward ("start running") did better than those who framed it as something to avoid ("stop eating sweets"): 58.9% against 47.1%. Success was self-reported and the goal type was not assigned at random, so read it as a strong hint, not proof.

There is also a reason January feels like a door. The researchers Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis called it the [fresh start effect](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901): searches for "diet", gym visits and goal commitments all rise after a new week, a birthday or a new year. A landmark lets us file our past mistakes in an earlier chapter.

So why do so many resolutions still fade? Often because they are aimed at an outcome while the person underneath stays the same. In [Atomic Habits](https://jamesclear.com/atomic-habits) (2018), James Clear argues that lasting change starts with identity. His advice in the [chapter on identity-based habits](https://jamesclear.com/identity-based-habits) is short: "Decide the type of person you want to be." Then prove it with small wins.

That is the difference between a resolution and an intention:

| Resolution | Intention |
|---|---|
| A target: lose ten kilos, quit, earn more | A direction: live with more care, more honesty, more presence |
| Measured by results, so one slip feels like failure | Lived day by day, so a slip is a chance to return |
| Often comes from pressure or guilt | Comes from what you saw clearly about yourself |
| "I will stop..." | "I am becoming someone who..." |

An intention can still lead to concrete habits. It simply gives them a root.

## Closing the year: what to let go of

Before you can welcome anything new, it helps to look honestly at what this year asked of you. For some it was a divorce, a death, a job that ended. For others nothing dramatic happened, and that is the problem: a sense of being stuck, fine on paper and empty inside.

The Buddhist teacher Pema Chödrön wrote [When Things Fall Apart](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL8697447M) (1997) for exactly these moments. "Things falling apart is a kind of testing and also a kind of healing," she writes, in a passage collected by [Maria Popova in The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2017/07/17/when-things-fall-apart-pema-chodron/). The healing, she says, comes from letting there be room for all of it: grief, relief, misery and joy.

Some questions to sit with in the last days of December:

- What did this year end that I have not yet said goodbye to?
- What am I still carrying that is not mine to carry: a resentment, a role, an old story about myself?
- Which habit kept me from being present, and what was it protecting me from?
- What am I grateful for, even in the hard parts?

You do not need answers to all of them. One honest sentence is enough to begin.

## Release rituals: fire, writing and water

### Why a ritual and not just a decision

The Harvard Business School professor Michael Norton studies rituals. In his book [The Ritual Effect](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL51646990M) (2024) he separates a habit, the what, from a ritual, the how: the care and meaning we put into an act. In [a long interview with Dr Rangan Chatterjee](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D1QIifozSOE), he explains why big transitions need a ceremony, so that "we have this before and after period."

With Francesca Gino, Norton tested this in the lab. In their [2014 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031772), people who had just lost a draw for $200 either drew their feelings, or drew them, sprinkled salt on the drawing, tore it up and counted to ten. The ritual group felt less grief and more in control, as [HBS Working Knowledge reported](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-power-of-rituals-in-life-death-and-business). It worked even for people who did not believe in rituals.

Most of the rituals people described in that research were private, not religious. One woman took the photos of herself and her ex to the park where they had met and tore them up. Norton says there is no switch that "just says I'm over it." A ritual gives grief somewhere to go.

### Fire: the burning bowl ceremony

The burning bowl ceremony is one of the best known New Year release rituals. Unity, a spiritual movement that holds one every January as an alternative to resolutions, describes it in [a simple guide](https://unity.org/resources/articles/how-burning-bowl-ritual):

1. Name what you want to release and notice the feelings that come with it.
2. Write it on a piece of paper: a word, a name or a whole page.
3. In a safe place, burn the paper in a metal bowl, a fireplace or a fire pit.
4. Watch the smoke rise and let yourself feel whatever comes.
5. Say a few words of release.
6. When the ashes are cool, give them to the wind or to water.
7. Write down what you want to make room for.

"The purpose of the ceremony is to shift consciousness," the guide says. The fire is a symbol; the shift happens in you.

### Water and the body

Water closes the cycle that fire opens. Scattering ashes in a river, a shower taken with attention, or a cold plunge after a sweat all say the same thing in the body: what was is washed off. In Mexico the [temazcal](../blog/what-is-a-temazcal) is the old form of this, a steam bath often described as a return to the womb of the earth.

## Mexican ways of receiving the year

Mexico receives the year with a mix of joy and ritual. As [El Universal](https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/nacion/sociedad/ano-nuevo-2021-los-rituales-mas-populares-para-recibir-el-ano/) lists them, many families eat twelve grapes at the twelve chimes of midnight, one wish for each month. Some walk a suitcase around the block to call in travel. Others sweep the house to clear out the energy of the old year, or wear yellow for abundance and red for love.

The grapes came from Spain. [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographicla.com/historia/2024/12/ano-nuevo-por-que-se-comen-12-uvas-en-nochevieja) traces them to Spanish newspapers of 1882 and a large grape harvest in 1909. They are playful, and they hold a real idea: name what you hope for, one by one, out loud.

Older than any of these is the Mexica New Fire ceremony. Every 52 years, at the close of a calendar cycle, all fires were put out. In the days before, people threw away household utensils, clothing and hearth stones, and archaeologists from INAH have found pits of these discarded objects in Mexico City, [Mexico News Daily reported](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/news/remains-mexica-ceremony-mexico-city/). Then a new fire was lit on the hill now called Cerro de la Estrella and carried back to the city.

The last one in Mexica times was held in 1507. The ceremony was revived in Iztapalapa in 1975, and by that count, [México Desconocido notes](https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/ceremonia-del-fuego-nuevo-iztapalapa.html), the 52-year cycle closes in 2027. Whether or not you count it that way, the gesture is worth keeping: put out the old fire, clear the house, light a new one.

## A simple New Year intention ritual you can do at home

You do not need a retreat for this. You need an hour, a candle, paper and some honesty. Here is a ritual you can do on the night of December 31, alone or with people you trust.

1. **Clear the space.** Sweep or tidy one room, the Mexican way, and turn off screens.
2. **Light a candle.** Sit for five minutes and breathe. The teacher Tara Brach, in [a short New Year meditation](https://www.tarabrach.com/meditation-what-matters/), invites you to ask what matters to your heart: "Entering a new year is a wonderful opportunity to clarify our purpose."
3. **Write what you release.** One page: what ended, what you are tired of carrying, what you forgive.
4. **Burn it safely.** Use a metal bowl outside or a fireplace, with water at hand. Watch it go.
5. **Give the ashes away.** When cool, scatter them in a garden or pour them into running water.
6. **Write your intention.** One sentence that begins "I am becoming someone who...". Keep it where you will see it.
7. **Choose one small action.** One habit that proves the intention, for the first week of January.
8. **Close with gratitude.** Name three things the old year gave you, even the hard ones.

Come back to your sentence on the first of each month. An intention is not set once; you return to it, again and again.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a spiritual way to celebrate New Year?**
Close the old year before you open the new one. Spend some quiet time reflecting, use a release ritual such as writing and burning what you let go of, then set an intention about who you want to become. It can be done alone, with family, or in ceremony.

**What is a burning bowl ceremony?**
It is a release ritual in which you write down what you want to let go of and burn the paper in a bowl or fire, then set intentions for the new year. Unity churches hold it each January as an alternative to resolutions. At home, do it outdoors or in a fireplace, with water close by.

**What is the difference between a New Year intention and a resolution?**
A resolution is a target, such as losing weight or quitting something. An intention is a direction, a way you want to live. Research suggests goals framed as moving toward something do better than goals about avoiding something, and an intention is naturally a "toward".

**Can I join a New Year retreat in Mexico if I have never sat in ceremony?**
