If you want to know how to change habits after a psychedelic experience, start here: the weeks after a ceremony are a window, not the change itself. Pick one or two small actions that come straight from what you saw, tie each one to something you already do every day, and repeat them for months. Change the cues and the company around the old habit, and plan for slips before they happen.

This guide is for the person who came home clear and determined, and is now watching the old routine creep back. We bring together what habit research, people in recovery and our own guests have found about making an insight last.

## The afterglow is a window, not the change

Many people come home lighter. Researchers call it the "afterglow": a period of better mood, more openness to yourself and others, and sometimes a strong sense of clarity and meaning. A [2025 study in the Journal of Psychopharmacology](https://doi.org/10.1177/02698811251326937) that built a questionnaire to measure it describes these effects as temporary.

That is the honest part. The glow fades. A [2025 review chapter on the afterglow window](https://doi.org/10.1007/7854_2025_612) adds that not everyone feels it, and some people struggle to make sense of what happened or to bring it into daily life.

So treat the glow as an open door. While you feel open, old habits feel looser and new ones feel possible. That is the best time to start, but what lasts is what you repeat after the door starts to close.

Todd Youngs, who used heroin, benzodiazepines and alcohol for more than twenty years and was close to ten years sober when he [wrote for Chacruna](https://chacruna.net/ayahuasca-and-the-twelve-steps-an-anonymous-friendship/), put it this way: "Ayahuasca is an engine that needs a transmission for its benefits and blessings to endure." The ceremony gives the push. Daily life is where the gears are.

## Why old patterns pull you back after ayahuasca

You saw the pattern clearly in ceremony. Then you walked into your kitchen and reached for the same thing at the same hour. That is not weakness. It is how habits work.

Psychiatrist Judson Brewer, who studies mindfulness and addiction, explains it in his [TEDMED talk on breaking bad habits](https://www.ted.com/talks/judson_brewer_a_simple_way_to_break_a_bad_habit) as "Trigger, behavior, reward." The brain stores the loop as a memory tied to a context, and later learns to use the same behavior to soothe sadness or stress.

Psychologist Wendy Wood, author of *Good Habits, Bad Habits*, makes the point about places in an [interview with Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/?p=21433): "We are all dependent on the context in which we live." She has found that a move can break a habit when it removes the cues, and that habits survive when the new place keeps them.

A retreat is a week without your cues. Home is full of them: the couch, the drive past the liquor store, the friend who calls on Fridays. In a [global survey of 1,630 ayahuasca drinkers](https://doi.org/10.3390/psychoactives2020014), going back to the "old life" with new understanding was one of the main integration challenges people described.

### The pain under the habit

Physician and author Gabor Maté writes about this in his book *In the Realm of Hungry Ghosts*, where he describes addiction as a way to soothe "deep-seated fears and discomforts". On [The Tim Ferriss Show](https://tim.blog/2018/06/04/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-dr-gabor-mate/) he said the useful question is not "why the addiction but why the pain".

Some people meet that pain in ceremony. Seeing it does not remove it. If the habit was doing a job, such as calming you, numbing grief or filling an empty evening, something else needs to do that job now.

## How to change habits after a psychedelic experience: start small

The most common mistake after a retreat is trying to change everything at once: new diet, daily meditation, no phone, a new job, all in the first week. It feels right in the glow. Within a month most of it is gone.

Start smaller. James Clear's *Atomic Habits* is built on "tiny, easy changes" repeated until they stick. Habit research agrees on the time it takes. In a [2010 study in the European Journal of Social Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674), people repeated one new daily behavior tied to a cue. As the [British Psychological Society summarized](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit), it took an average of 66 days to become automatic, with a range from 18 days to a predicted 254.

So think in months, not in the week after you land.

### Turn an insight into a habit

| An insight (example) | A small daily habit | Attach it to |
|---|---|---|
| "I drink to stop feeling lonely in the evening" | Call or message one person | Getting home from work |
| "I never stop to feel anything" | Five minutes of silence | First coffee or tea |
| "I have been hard on my body" | A 10-minute walk outside | After lunch |
| "I keep things to myself" | Three lines in a journal | Getting into bed |

A few steps that help:

1. **Write down your two or three clearest insights** in the first days home, in your own words.
2. **Choose one, at most two,** and turn each into an action small enough to do on your worst day.
3. **Attach it to a cue** you already have: a meal, a commute, brushing your teeth.
4. **Make the old habit harder.** Wood found that people with good self-control mostly arrange their lives so there is less temptation to resist. Remove the bottle, delete the app, change the route.
5. **Review once a week** with someone who knows what you are working on.

## Change who you are, not only what you do

In a chapter of *Atomic Habits* that Clear [shares on his site](https://jamesclear.com/identity-based-habits), he argues that lasting habits start with identity: decide who you want to be, then collect small proof. "In order to believe in a new identity, we have to prove it to ourselves."

This is where a ceremony can help in a way a resolution rarely does. In one study, 15 long-term smokers took psilocybin inside a structured 15-week quit program with counselling. In that [small open-label pilot](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881114548296), 12 of the 15 were not smoking at six months. In the [long-term follow-up](https://doi.org/10.3109/00952990.2016.1170135), 9 were still abstinent at an average of 30 months.

It is a small study without a control group, about tobacco, and the medicine was only one part of the program. What matters here is what the participants said. When researchers [interviewed them about 30 months later](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881118780612), they described vivid insights into who they were and why they smoked. They also named the preparatory counselling, their bond with the study team and the momentum of the program as vital to staying off cigarettes.

Brewer describes the same move from the other side. One of his smokers, after smoking with full attention, went "from knowing in her head" that smoking was bad for her "to knowing it in her bones". Ceremony can give that kind of knowing. The daily habit is how you keep living from it.

If you are curious about what may be happening in the brain during these weeks, we wrote about [psilocybin and neuroplasticity](../blog/psilocybin-neuroplasticity). The short version: research is early, and openness to change still needs practice to become a new way of living.

## Community, people and places

You cannot keep a new habit alone in an old environment. In the [Life after Ayahuasca survey](https://doi.org/10.3390/psychoactives2020014), the tools people found most useful were a like-minded community and ongoing practices such as yoga, meditation and journaling. The authors suggest that community and the body may matter as much as talk.

The ICEERS foundation says in its [guide to integration](https://www.iceers.org/art-integration-support-ayahuasca-experiences/) that "the experience does not end when the ceremony concludes, but when the community receives the participant again." Maté learned this the hard way: his own first ceremony had, in his words, "no processing afterwards".

Some ways to build that support:

- **Keep in touch with your retreat group.** Brett Lemker, who sat with us, wrote in his [review on BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/r/7-day-ayahuasca-retreat-at-an-authentic-finca-in-mexico): "I arrived as a stranger among 16 attendees and left with 16 lifelong friends."
- **Find a weekly circle**: a meditation group, a men's or women's group, a recovery meeting, a yoga class you attend with the same people.
- **Tell one or two people at home** what you are changing and how they can help. Choose people who listen well.
- **Look honestly at the places and people tied to the old habit.** You do not have to cut everyone off. You may need to change where and when you see them.

During the retreat we hold group and one-on-one integration. After the retreat we stay in touch, and the group usually does too. Our page on [preparing for your retreat](../prepare) covers the first days home, and [what to expect in the days after ayahuasca](../blog/after-ayahuasca-integration) covers sleep, emotions and food.

## When you slip

You will. Plan for it now, while you feel strong.

The good news from habit research is that one miss does not erase your progress. In the Lally study, [a single missed day had little impact](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit) on how automatic the habit became. Many missed days in a row did.

So keep the rule simple: if you miss a day, do the small habit again the next day. One skipped walk on Saturday matters much less than a skipped month.

Meet the slip with curiosity instead of shame. Brewer's work is built on this: notice what the craving feels like, what set it off, what it actually gave you. Shame tends to feed the loop. Curiosity tends to loosen it.

At MexiSol World we say there are no good or bad ceremonies, only experiences. The weeks after are the same. A slip is information about a cue you missed or a need that is still unmet. As Youngs wrote, "Integration is less about talking than it is about actually doing." The doing includes starting again.

### A word on addiction

A retreat is not rehab or detox, and we do not promise sobriety. Guests must arrive sober, and active dependence on alcohol, benzodiazepines or opioids can mean a retreat is not safe for you, as our [medical information](../medical) explains. Medical screening is required for everyone, and any change to a prescribed medication is made only with the doctor who prescribed it.

If you are working on drinking or drug use, keep the support that already works for you, whether that is a counselor, a doctor or a program. A ceremony may give you a new view of the pain behind the habit. The work of changing it happens at home, with people around you.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does the psychedelic afterglow last?**
Researchers describe it as temporary, and studies measure it in the first weeks after a session. It varies from person to person, and some people do not feel it at all. Use it to start a habit, but do not count on it to keep one going.

**How long does it take to form a new habit after a retreat?**
In the best-known study, it took an average of 66 days for a daily habit to become automatic, with a range from 18 to about 254 days. Expect two to several months of steady practice.

**Can a retreat help me stop drinking?**
We do not offer treatment for addiction, and we cannot promise any outcome. In a small smoking study, participants described clear insights into why they smoked, but research on psychedelics and addiction is still small and early. You need to arrive alcohol free, pass medical screening, and keep professional support at home if you have it.

**What should I do if I go back to old patterns after ayahuasca?**
Start the small habit again the next day, and get curious about what triggered the slip. Talk to someone from your group or your support circle. One slip does not undo the work.

**What is the best daily practice after a retreat?**
The one you will actually do. Yoga, meditation and journaling are among the practices that people in the Life after Ayahuasca survey found helpful. Keep it small enough to do on a hard day.
