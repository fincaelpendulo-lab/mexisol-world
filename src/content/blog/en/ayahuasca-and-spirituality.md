Ayahuasca and spirituality have been tied together for as long as the brew has been known. In the Amazon it is called a teacher and a doctor, and in Brazil it is the sacrament of whole churches. For many people who drink it, it brings a direct sense of the sacred: unity, love, a connection with nature and the feeling of remembering something they had forgotten. That is what people mean by an opening of consciousness.

This article is for those who already know the ceremony, or have read a lot about it, and want to go deeper. We look at the traditions, at how thinkers and researchers have described the experience, and at the part that matters most: how that opening becomes a daily practice once you are home.

## Why people call ayahuasca a spiritual teacher

The anthropologist Luis Eduardo Luna has studied ayahuasca since the 1970s. In the Peruvian Amazon he worked with mestizo healers called vegetalistas, who say they learned their craft from the plants themselves. In [an essay for Cultural Survival](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/publications/cultural-survival-quarterly/ayahuasca-shamanism-shared-across-cultures) he writes that they call these plants "doctores, or plant teachers."

In his [2024 keynote at Harvard Divinity School](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mSEerfVMs9s), Luna recalled his own teacher, Don Emilio Andrade Gómez. For Don Emilio, ayahuasca was a "doctor," and so was tobacco. Health and wisdom were "two sides of the same coin." The visions, Luna said, were not the main point.

That is a useful correction for anyone who has sat in ceremony before. The tradition does not treat ayahuasca as a show of colors. It treats it as a relationship with something that teaches, if you are willing to listen.

## Ayahuasca and spirituality in Amazonian and syncretic traditions

There is no single ayahuasca religion. The brew has been used in ceremony across the Amazon for generations, and today there are many ways of drinking, each with its own songs, rules and meaning.

| Tradition | Where and when | How it frames the brew |
|---|---|---|
| Vegetalismo | Mestizo healers, mostly in Peru | Plant teachers ("doctores") who give the healer knowledge, mainly for healing |
| Santo Daime | Founded by Raimundo Irineu Serra in Rio Branco, Acre, Brazil | The central sacrament, with a doctrine sung in hymns and ritual |
| Barquinha | Founded by Daniel Pereira de Matos, also in Rio Branco | A church whose cosmology centers on spirits living in plants and animals |
| União do Vegetal | Created in 1961 by José Gabriel da Costa | A sacrament in a structured church that calls the brew "vegetal" |

Luna's essay, the source for the table, notes that all the ayahuasqueros he met considered themselves Christians and might invoke Jesus or Mary in their rituals. In Brazil, he writes, Amazonian traditions met Afro-Brazilian religion and European esoteric ideas, and the churches were born from that meeting.

Why does this matter for you? Because the setting shapes what the medicine means. In a church it is a sacrament; with a vegetalista it is a teacher and healer. Our ayahuasca ceremonies are led by Humberto, who trained in Peru through long master plant dietas, and our brew is prepared in Pucallpa. The roots are Amazonian, even though we gather in Jalisco.

## What the opening of consciousness feels like

People struggle to put it into words, and that difficulty is part of it.

In a [2009 qualitative study from Karlstad University](https://doi.org/10.1080/02791072.2009.10399767), 25 Northern Europeans wrote anonymous accounts of their ayahuasca sessions. The researchers found six recurring themes, which they called the "transcendental circle." It often began with a frightening, narrow state, then a sudden shift, then what they called limitless, expansive states.

In those expansive states, people described:

- **Unity.** The insight that "all is one," felt in the body, not just thought.
- **Plant teachers.** Encounters with the spirit of ayahuasca, of trees and other plants, which "functioned as teachers."
- **Love.** One participant wrote: "maybe love just is!"
- **Remembering.** The new view of life often felt like something "previously known but had somehow been forgotten."

It is a small study of self-selected people, so it describes experiences rather than proving anything. But the words are familiar to anyone who has sat through a long night.

Our own guests have written about it in their public reviews. Jermaine Thomas wrote on [BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/r/7-day-ayahuasca-retreat-at-an-authentic-finca-in-mexico-69537): "The love that permeates this space is other worldly and each connection that I made with each person has felt familiar." Another guest, signed Anonymous Yogi, wrote: "I had cathartic reactions that opened up my desires to be less judgmental, and more open to love for myself and others."

Notice the second account. The opening was not only a vision. It was a change in how that person wanted to treat themselves and others.

## How thinkers and researchers describe ayahuasca and consciousness

### William James: beyond words, and full of knowledge

In 1902 the psychologist William James gave lectures later published as [The Varieties of Religious Experience](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/621). He named two marks of a mystical state. The first is ineffability: it "defies expression" and has to be lived to be known. The second is a noetic quality: it feels like knowledge, insight "into depths of truth unplumbed by the discursive intellect."

James never drank ayahuasca. Yet his two marks describe what many people report the morning after: I cannot explain it, and I know it was true.

### Stanislav Grof: experience, not belief

The psychiatrist Stanislav Grof spent decades studying non-ordinary states. In [a short talk for Science and Nonduality](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=n6yiMA2JXnY) he called psychedelics "catalysts." As you go to the roots of your problems, he said, "sooner or later the spiritual dimension in the psyche opens." For him it is "a direct experience," one that "has nothing to do with belief."

### Benny Shanon and Michael Pollan

The cognitive psychologist Benny Shanon wrote [The Antipodes of the Mind](https://academic.oup.com/book/47914) (Oxford University Press, 2002), one of the most detailed maps of the ayahuasca state. He built it from his own sessions and from interviews with indigenous people, shamans, church members and travellers, with whole chapters on consciousness, meaning and light.

The journalist Michael Pollan came at it from the outside. In [How to Change Your Mind](https://michaelpollan.com/books/how-to-change-your-mind/) (2018) and in [a talk at the Royal Institution](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=40Os_t0LG94), he describes a woman in a psilocybin study, an atheist, who felt "bathed in God's love." Asked why an atheist would say that, she replied that "we don't have any word big enough." Different medicine, same problem James named.

### What the research on church members found

A [longitudinal study published in PLoS ONE in 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0042421) compared 127 long-term members of Brazilian ayahuasca churches with 115 people active in other religions. The ayahuasca users scored higher on self-transcendence and on every part of a spiritual orientation scale, and those differences were still there at the follow-up about a year later.

The authors are clear about the limits. It is an observational study, people who had a hard time may have left the churches, and the answers are self-reported. It does not show that ayahuasca makes people spiritual. It does show that for people who drink it within a spiritual community over many years, the spiritual side stays central.

## Intention, surrender and the ceremony as a sacred space

The tradition and the research agree on one thing: what you bring and where you are shape what opens.

**Intention.** On the second day of our ayahuasca week, everyone sets intentions with Humberto before the first ceremony. An intention is not a demand. It is a direction, a question you are willing to have answered in a way you did not expect.

**Surrender.** Pollan describes the advice given in research settings: "to surrender," and not to resist. The Karlstad study found that the frightening phase often came before the opening. At MexiSol World we say there are no good or bad experiences, only experiences. Hard moments are part of the process, and they are often where the teaching is.

**The space.** Our three night ceremonies, two with ayahuasca and one with Satori, take place in the Osho Hut at Finca El Péndulo, near Tepatitlán. Humberto opens each one and sings through the night with live musicians. Servers bring water and a steady arm. That holding is what makes surrender possible.

## Ayahuasca spiritual awakening: bringing the opening home

An opening that stays in the ceremony room fades. James noted this too: mystical states are transient. What lasts is what you practise.

A London dentist who wrote about a ceremony in England put it simply in [a first-person piece for VICE](https://www.vice.com/en/article/i-took-ayahuasca-at-a-countryside-retreat-and-it-was-as-profound-as-they-say-it-is-v25n4/): "And I was not alone. And I never would be again." The writer also expected the visions to fade, and saw that "integrating what you learn into your life" is part of the work.

Some practices that help turn an expanded consciousness into a spiritual life:

1. **Write it down the next morning.** Not to explain it, but to keep the words before they slip away.
2. **Choose one thing.** Pick a single insight, such as being less judgmental or more patient, and live it for a month.
3. **Keep a daily sit.** Ten minutes of silence, prayer or breath keeps the door from closing.
4. **Return to nature.** Many people come home with a new tie to plants and the land. Walk, garden, sit outside.
5. **Share with people who can listen.** Integration circles, a teacher or a trusted friend.

During the week we hold group and one-on-one integration, a fire circle, a temazcal and a closing cacao ceremony, and we stay in touch after you go home. Our guide on [what to expect in the days after ayahuasca](../blog/after-ayahuasca-integration) covers the first weeks in detail.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is ayahuasca a spiritual experience?**
For many people, yes. In the Amazon it is treated as a teacher, and in Brazil it is the sacrament of several churches. People often describe unity, love and a sense of the sacred, though every ceremony is different and some nights are quiet.

**What does it mean that ayahuasca opens consciousness?**
People describe seeing beyond their usual sense of self: feeling connected to everything, understanding something they cannot put into words, or remembering what matters. William James called these marks ineffability and a noetic quality. Whether the opening lasts depends on how you live it afterwards.

**Do I need to be religious to drink ayahuasca in ceremony?**
No. Our guests come from many faiths and from none. What helps is openness, a clear intention and willingness to surrender to the process.

**Is ego dissolution the goal of an ayahuasca ceremony?**
We do not set any particular experience as the goal. Some people feel the self dissolve, others work on one memory all night. The tradition sees ayahuasca as a teacher, and teachers choose the lesson.
