Ayahuasca and grief have a long history together. People who sit in ceremony after a loss often describe crying they could not do before, vivid memories of the person, and for some, a sense of meeting them again to say goodbye. Small studies have found that grief tends to ease after ceremony, mostly through acceptance, but ayahuasca does not take grief away, and it is not meant to.

At MexiSol World we see grief as something you go through, not something to fix. This article is for anyone who lost someone and feels stuck: what a ceremony can open, what the research saw, what others have said in their own words, and how to carry it home. Our retreats take place at Finca El Péndulo, near Tepatitlán in Jalisco.

## Grief is something to go through, not a problem to fix

Most of us were never taught how to grieve. We were taught to be strong, keep busy, and get back to normal as fast as possible.

Psychotherapist Francis Weller has spent decades working with grief. In a [2025 conversation with Tami Simon of Sounds True](https://resources2.soundstrue.com/podcast/francis-weller-our-apprenticeship-with-sorrow/) he put it plainly: "We're not taught how to work with grief, how to accompany it, how to befriend it." In his book [The Wild Edge of Sorrow](https://www.northatlanticbooks.com/shop/the-wild-edge-of-sorrow/) (2015) he describes five gates of grief. The first is the one everyone meets sooner or later: "everything we love, we will lose."

Stephen Jenkinson led a palliative care counselling team at Mount Sinai Hospital in Toronto and sat at the deathbeds of more than 1,000 people, as the documentary [Griefwalker](https://www.nfb.ca/film/griefwalker/) (National Film Board of Canada, 2008) shows. He goes further. In a [2025 interview with Nate Hagens](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XgWpq66LuWY) he says the skill is "the mourning that you don't try to accelerate through." And then: "Grief is your companion now, not your adversary."

That is the frame we bring to ceremony. You are not coming to finish grieving. You are coming to stop running from it, so it can move.

## What people describe feeling in an ayahuasca ceremony after a loss

Every ceremony is different, and nobody can promise what you will see or feel. But some themes come up again and again in published accounts and in research.

In a [2017 study in the journal OMEGA](https://doi.org/10.1177/0030222817710879), researchers asked bereaved people to describe their ayahuasca experiences in their own words. Three themes stood out:

- **Emotional release.** Tears that had been stuck for months or years.
- **Biographical memories.** Scenes from life with the person, sometimes long forgotten.
- **Contact with the deceased.** A sense of being with them again.

According to a [press release from ICEERS](https://www.iceers.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Ayahuasca-en-procesos-de-duelo-Nota-de-prensa.pdf), the foundation behind the study, about a third of those drinkers reported meeting the "presence", "soul" or "essence" of their loved one. The same release calls ayahuasca by one of its old names: the vine of the dead.

We do not tell anyone what those encounters are. Some people feel they truly met their mother or son. Others feel they met their own love for that person, finally allowed to come up. Both can change how you carry the loss.

Not every night is gentle. Grief can arrive as anger, guilt, or a heavy body. We see no good or bad ceremonies, only experiences, and the hard ones are part of the work.

## What research on ayahuasca and grief found: acceptance and decentering

The most cited study is a [2020 prospective study in Psychopharmacology](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-019-05446-2) by Débora González and colleagues. They followed bereaved people who attended Shipibo ayahuasca ceremonies at a center in Peru, an average of 6.6 ceremonies each.

| What they measured | What they found |
|---|---|
| Who took part | 50 people at the start, 27 still answering at 12 months |
| Grief severity | Lower at 15 days, 3, 6 and 12 months after the retreat |
| Self-report | 92.3% of the 39 who answered at 15 days said ayahuasca had a positive effect on their grief |
| What went with less grief | Less avoidance of painful feelings (acceptance), and more decentering |

Two of those words matter for anyone who is stuck.

**Acceptance** here means no longer pushing away the reality of the death and the pain that comes with it. In the press release, González put the main finding simply: ayahuasca "facilitates the acceptance of the death of a loved one" (our translation).

**Decentering** means being able to watch your own thoughts and feelings without being swallowed by them. The grief is still there, but you are no longer only the grief.

### What the research cannot tell us yet

This evidence is early. The 2020 study had no control group, it was small, and nine participants also had psychotherapy during the year. The authors say themselves that without a placebo group they cannot know whether ayahuasca caused the change.

A [2025 trial in Scientific Reports](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-13251-5) adds an honest detail. It compared 84 people who had lost a close relative in the past year: ayahuasca plus a grief therapy called meaning reconstruction, the therapy alone, or no treatment. All three groups improved, including the people who did nothing. The ayahuasca group improved most, with no serious adverse events, but the study was not randomized and the authors call it preliminary.

The lesson we take from it: grief moves with time, care and company. Ceremony can be one place where it moves. It is not the only one.

## Published stories of ayahuasca after losing a parent

People have written about this in their own words. Here are two accounts, quoted from the original articles.

### Prince Harry: "the misery of loss"

In a [2023 60 Minutes interview reported by CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/prince-harry-psychedelics-60-minutes-2023-01-09/), Anderson Cooper asked Prince Harry about ayahuasca and psilocybin. Harry said that for years he was never able to cry for his mother, though he kept trying.

"For me, they cleared the windscreen, the windshield, the misery of loss," he said. They also cleared the idea that he needed to cry to prove he missed her, "when in fact, all she wanted was for me to be happy." He added a warning worth repeating: "I would never recommend people to do this recreationally."

### Celia Peachey: "something was breathing for me"

In a [first-person account in VICE](https://www.vice.com/en/article/how-tripping-on-ayahuasca-helped-me-process-my-mums-murder-284/) (2015), Celia Peachey described ayahuasca ceremonies in Spain, in a psychotherapeutic setting, after her mother was killed. During one ceremony, she wrote, "It was like something was breathing for me." About two weeks later she said: "I have cleared my sadness and freed myself from a lot of grief."

These are single stories, told soon after. They are not a promise. They show what people can find when grief finally has room.

## Rituals for the dead: from Día de Muertos to ceremony

This post goes live a couple of weeks after Día de Muertos. In Mexico we already live with a grief ritual of our own.

[UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/indigenous-festivity-dedicated-to-the-dead-00054), which inscribed it as intangible heritage in 2008, describes it as celebrating the "transitory return to Earth of deceased relatives and loved ones." Families lay petals and candles from the cemetery to the home, and set the dead person's favorite food on the altar.

Notice what this ritual does. It does not ask you to get over anyone. It sets a place for them. Grief is shared, out loud, with food and music and the whole family.

Weller says the same in his own way: "The main thing is to not think of it in isolation." Ceremony works on a similar principle. You drink in a circle, with people you came to know that week, with songs carrying the night.

## What a spiritual retreat for grief looks like with us

Our ayahuasca retreat is a full week. These are the parts that matter most when you arrive carrying a loss:

1. **Screening first.** Medical screening is required. We review your medications, and any change to a prescribed medication is made only with your prescribing doctor. If you take antidepressants or sleep medication, please read our [medical information](../medical) early.
2. **Your intention.** We talk about why you are coming. You might start by saying the name of the person you lost, out loud.
3. **Three night ceremonies** in the Osho Hut: two with ayahuasca and one with Satori, led by Humberto, who sings through the night with live musicians.
4. **The mornings after.** Group integration with Humberto, and one-on-one time during the week. Our integration coach is a woman, and most of our team are women.
5. **Fire and water.** A temazcal and a fire circle during the week give the body its own way to let go. Read more about [the temazcal](../blog/what-is-a-temazcal).

This is not grief therapy, and we do not offer it as a substitute for one. If your grief has become months of not functioning, please also work with a grief counsellor or therapist. Ceremony and therapy can sit side by side. If you are unsure whether you are in the right moment, [is ayahuasca right for me?](../blog/is-ayahuasca-right-for-me) can help you decide.

## Carrying it home

The ceremony ends. The grief does not, and that is fine. Jenkinson's word for it is companion.

A few practices help grief keep moving once you are home:

- **Write it down** the morning after each ceremony, especially anything the person said or showed you.
- **Make a small altar** at home: a photo, a candle, a flower. Sit with it for a few minutes a day.
- **Say their name** to someone who knew them. Grief needs witnesses.
- **Go slow.** Weller reminds us that "ritual doesn't have to be complicated."
- **Stay in contact.** We stay in touch after you go home, and integration often takes two to three months.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can ayahuasca help with grief?**
Small studies found that grief became less severe after ceremony, mostly through more acceptance and less avoidance. The evidence is early, without placebo controls, and grief also eases with time on its own. People describe release, memories and a sense of goodbye, but nobody can promise what you will experience.

**Will I see or talk to the person I lost?**
Some people do. In one study about a third of bereaved drinkers described meeting the presence of their loved one. Many others do not, and still find the grief moves. Come with an intention, not an expectation.

**How soon after a death should I do ayahuasca?**
There is no fixed rule. In the first weeks most people need rest, family and practical support more than a ceremony. Talk with us about where you are, and if you take medication, start the screening early so your prescribing doctor has time.

**Is ayahuasca a replacement for grief counselling?**
No. A retreat is a week of ceremony and integration, not therapy. If grief has kept you from working, sleeping or eating for months, please see a professional too. The two can work well together.
