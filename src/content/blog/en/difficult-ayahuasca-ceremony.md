If you are scared of a bad ayahuasca experience, you are not alone, and the fear makes sense. Some nights are hard: fear, grief, nausea, old memories. What people say afterwards, and what the research suggests, is that a hard night is often where the most important work happens, especially when you stop fighting it and have people around you who know how to hold it.

At MexiSol World we say there are no good or bad ceremonies, only experiences. This article explains why, what a difficult night can feel like, what surrender means in practice, and how we care for you when it gets heavy. Our retreats take place at Finca El Péndulo, near Tepatitlán in Jalisco.

## Why we say there is no bad ayahuasca experience

People search for "bad trip" because they want to know the worst that can happen. We understand that. But labeling a night as bad shuts the door on what it came to show you.

Gabor Maté, the physician and author who has written for decades about trauma and addiction, put it simply in a [long conversation with Tim Ferriss](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H9B5mYfBPlY): "Whatever experience you have, that's the experience you need to have." In the same talk he describes the trap many people fall into. "Some people sit through the whole night resisting their experience because it doesn't meet their expectations."

That is the difference between an intention and an expectation. An intention is a direction you bring. An expectation is a script you want the medicine to follow. Ayahuasca rarely follows scripts.

A guest who sat with us described it this way in a [review on BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/organizers/o/ruger#reviews): "Trust the process. The medicine has a way of finding what you need." (Sanjay, April 2025)

## What a difficult ayahuasca ceremony can feel like

Every night is different, and many are gentle. But it helps to know what a harder one can bring, so it does not catch you off guard.

- **Fear:** a sense that something is wrong, or that you are losing your mind. Science writer John Horgan described thinking, in [Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/cross-check/my-ayahuasca-trip/): "What if I go mad?"
- **Grief and old pain:** memories you had put away, losses you never mourned, the feeling of a younger self.
- **The body:** nausea, vomiting, heat or cold, heaviness. In the [Global Ayahuasca Survey](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgph.0000438) of more than 10,000 people, 62% reported vomiting or nausea. In ceremony it is understood as part of the cleanse.
- **Darkness:** images or feelings that seem frightening, or a sense of emptiness.
- **Time:** minutes that feel like hours.

None of this means the ceremony is going wrong. It usually means something is moving. If you want to know the full shape of a night, read [your first ayahuasca ceremony: what to expect](../blog/first-ayahuasca-ceremony).

## Control versus surrender: what ayahuasca asks of you

Most hard moments get harder when we fight them. Michael Pollan, who wrote [How to Change Your Mind](https://www.penguin.com.au/books/how-to-change-your-mind-9780141985138) (2018), spent years talking with researchers and guides. In a [talk at Google](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KuhmZSFvhL0) he summed up their advice in four words: "surrender to the experience. Trust."

He went further: "A bad trip is essentially a panic reaction against what's happening in your head." The panic, not the content, is what turns a heavy moment into a frightening one.

On [NPR's Fresh Air](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2018/05/15/611225541/reluctant-psychonaut-michael-pollan-embraces-the-new-science-of-psychedelics) he described the "flight instructions" guides give: "If you see a monster, for example, don't try to run away. Walk right up to it, plant your feet and say, 'What do you have to teach me?'" According to those instructions, the fear then tends to change into something kinder.

### What surrender looks like in the moment

Surrender is not giving up. It is a small, repeated choice to stop pushing away what is already here.

1. **Breathe out slowly.** A long exhale tells your body you are safe enough.
2. **Name it.** "This is fear." "This is grief." Naming gives you a little distance.
3. **Turn toward it.** Ask the question from the flight instructions: what do you have to teach me?
4. **Come back to the music.** Humberto sings icaros, the medicine songs, with live musicians all night. Many people use the songs as a rope to hold.
5. **Ask for help.** Raise your hand. Surrender does not mean doing it alone.

Horgan's account is honest about the other path. He kept watching his experience as material for a book, never quite letting go, and left the night more confused than before. Holding on is a choice too, and it has a cost.

## The ayahuasca dark night in spiritual traditions

The idea that darkness can be part of growth is very old. In the 16th century the Spanish mystic St. John of the Cross wrote of the [Dark Night of the Soul](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/john_cross/dark_night.html), a painful passage the soul goes through on the way to union with the divine. In his poem the night is not an enemy. He calls it a happy night, and writes "¡Oh noche que me guiaste!": O night that guided me.

In the 20th century psychiatrist Stanislav Grof and Christina Grof gathered psychologists and spiritual teachers in [Spiritual Emergency](https://www.penguin.com.au/books/spiritual-emergency-9780874775389) (1989). The book describes crises "when the process of growth and change becomes chaotic and overwhelming" and argues that, with support, they can become openings rather than breakdowns.

This is the frame we offer. A hard night is not proof that you are broken or that the medicine has turned against you. It can be the part of the work that the rest of your life was asking for.

## What the research says about challenging experiences

Science has started to look at this, and the picture is encouraging but not simple.

| Study | Who | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| [Carbonaro et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/0269881116662634), online survey | 1,993 people describing their most difficult psilocybin experience | 39% ranked it among the five most challenging experiences of their lives. 34% ranked it among the five most meaningful, and 76% said it increased their well-being. 11% put themselves or others at risk of physical harm. |
| [Bouso et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgph.0000438), Global Ayahuasca Survey | 10,836 ayahuasca drinkers | 55.9% reported difficult mental effects in the weeks or months after. Around 88% saw those effects as part of a positive process of growth or integration. |
| [Andión et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000097), reanalysis of the same survey | 5,400 people | Extreme fear during ceremony was the strongest predictor of struggling afterwards. Ceremonies people found spiritually meaningful were linked to better mental health. |
| [Gómez-Sousa et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1097/jcp.0000000000001343), case series | 7 of 40 first-time drinkers who had intense difficult reactions | Four improved and stayed better at six months. Two got worse. The authors point to poor setting, guiding and screening as contributing factors. |

A [summary in Chacruna](https://chacruna.net/a-phenomenology-of-subjectively-relevant-experiences-induced-by-ayahuasca-in-upper-amazon-vegetalismo-tourism/) of interviews with ayahuasca drinkers in the Upper Amazon adds a useful detail: "Unpleasant emotional states were often followed by pleasant ones." What seems to matter is whether the difficult part gets worked through.

These are surveys and small studies, mostly self-reported, and they cannot prove cause. But together they point the same way: a hard night can become meaningful, and the setting, the support and the integration afterwards make a real difference. That is why we take all three seriously.

## How we hold you when it gets hard

You are never alone in the ceremony. This is what that means in practice at MexiSol World.

- **Before you come:** medical screening is required. We review your medications (any change is made only with your prescribing doctor), talk about why you are coming, and about your commitment to the process. If you are wondering whether you are ready, [is ayahuasca right for me?](../blog/is-ayahuasca-right-for-me) is a good place to start.
- **Your dose:** decided in conversation, not by a fixed rule. You can choose a smaller cup and ask for more later. A second cup is always your choice.
- **During the night:** servers stay in the room the whole time. They bring water and tissues, help you to the bathroom with a steady arm, and sit with you when things get heavy. Humberto and the musicians hold the space with song.
- **The morning after:** group integration with Humberto, and one-on-one time during the week. Our integration coach is a woman, and most of our team are women.
- **At home:** we stay in touch after you leave.

## Making meaning of a hard night in the days after

The night ends, but the work does not. Maté warns in the same interview that without integration, an experience is "going to become a memory, a nice memory." The same is true for a hard one: without care, it can stay a wound instead of becoming a lesson.

A few things help:

- **Write it down** the next morning, before it fades. Include the frightening parts.
- **Ask what it was pointing to.** Not "why did this happen to me" but "what in my life looks like this?"
- **Talk about it** in group or one-on-one integration. Saying it out loud often changes it.
- **Be patient.** Meaning can arrive weeks later.

The same guest above wrote: "I had cathartic reactions that opened up my desires to be less judgmental, and more open to love for myself and others." Another guest, Jay Poindexter, wrote that the ceremonies "expanded my awareness and acceptance of my dark and light sides."

## Frequently asked questions

**What happens if I panic during an ayahuasca ceremony?**
Raise your hand and someone will come to you. Servers are in the room all night, and they can sit with you, help you breathe and bring you back to the music. Panic usually passes faster when you stop fighting it and let someone help.

**Can a bad ayahuasca experience cause lasting harm?**
Most difficult feelings pass in the hours or days after. In the Global Ayahuasca Survey, 55.9% reported difficult mental effects afterwards, and around 88% saw them as part of growth, but 11.9% sought professional support. This is why medical screening is required, and why integration matters. If you ever feel unsafe after a ceremony, contact us and local emergency services right away.

**Can I stop or leave if it gets too hard?**
The effects cannot be switched off once you drink, but you are never left alone with them. A server can sit with you, and the second cup is always your choice. You also choose your dose in conversation, and can start smaller.

**How do I prepare so a hard night is easier?**
Set a clear intention, not an expectation. Follow the diet, rest, and come with a willingness to meet whatever shows up. Our guide on [how to prepare for ayahuasca](../blog/how-to-prepare-for-ayahuasca) covers the practical side.

**Is it normal to feel scared of ayahuasca before going?**
Yes. Many people feel some fear before a ceremony, especially the first time. Fear before is not a bad sign. It often means you are taking it seriously.
