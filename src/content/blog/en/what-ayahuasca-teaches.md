What does ayahuasca teach you? People who drink it in ceremony most often come home with lessons about themselves: that they are more than the story they have been telling, that forgiveness is possible, that they are connected to others, and that they know less than they thought. None of these lessons are new. What changes is that you feel them, instead of only understanding them.

If you have spent years in therapy, read the books and still feel that something is missing, this matters. The question is not only what the medicine shows you. It is whether you bring that lesson home and live it.

## Why ayahuasca is called a teacher plant

The idea comes from the Amazon. In 1984 the anthropologist Luis Eduardo Luna published [a study of four mestizo healers in Iquitos, Peru](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-8741(84)90036-9). These healers, called vegetalistas, work with plants they call "doctores" or plant teachers. They believe that if they keep a period of isolation and a strict diet, the plants "teach" them how to diagnose, how to heal and which songs to sing.

The anthropologist Jeremy Narby has spent decades with Amazonian peoples. In [a 2021 interview with Makery](https://www.makery.info/en/2021/04/13/english-plant-teachers-radical-social-distancing-and-falling-skies-interview-with-jeremy-narby/) he explained that they "consider them as teachers," and that people who drink them "can get knowledge, and gain insights and understandings about their current interests."

Narby first wrote about this in [The Cosmic Serpent](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/349746/the-cosmic-serpent-by-jeremy-narby/9780874779646) (1998), which asks how Amazonian healers say they gain knowledge through visionary plants. Later he wrote [Plant Teachers](https://newworldlibrary.com/product/plant-teachers) (2021) together with Rafael Chanchari Pizuri, an elder and healer of the Shawi people. Stephan Beyer's [Singing to the Plants](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL15468476W) (2009) describes the same world from the inside: how apprentice healers relate to the spirits of the plants.

A teacher, in this sense, is not someone who hands you answers. It is a relationship. You bring a question, you listen, and you keep the conditions that make listening possible.

## What does ayahuasca teach you? What people report

There is little research on this exact question, but one study asked it directly. In 2015, psychologists at Palacký University in the Czech Republic [surveyed 77 people who had travelled to South America to drink ayahuasca](https://doi.org/10.1080/02791072.2015.1094590). Many went looking for self-knowledge, spiritual growth or direction in life.

The benefits they named most often were:

- **Self-knowledge**
- **A change in the way they relate to themselves**
- **Spiritual development**
- **Better relationships with other people**
- **A new perspective on life**

It is a small survey of self-selected people, so it tells us what people report, not what will happen to you. But the list matches what we hear, and what the accounts below describe. Notice that most of it is not about visions at all.

Narby made the same point in [a talk at the 2019 World Ayahuasca Conference](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jAGdBhi1pZw). Indigenous Amazonian people, he said, "tend to say that those visuals are not the essential part." What matters is the purge and "the revelations, the understanding, the ideas."

## Lesson one: you are not your story

Most of us carry a sentence about ourselves that we stopped questioning long ago. I am too much. I am not enough. Nobody stays.

The psychologist Adèle Lafrance, writing for [Chacruna](https://chacruna.net/ayahuasca-and-the-power-of-forgiveness/), shares the words of a woman after her first ceremony. She saw that her mother had been emotionally absent because that was how her mother coped with her own pain. "She wasn't ignoring me because I was unlovable, which is the story I had been telling myself."

The physician Gabor Maté described something similar on [The Tim Ferriss Show](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H9B5mYfBPlY). In his first ceremony he saw "all of the ways that I had closed my heart against love." He understood why: when he was a year old, his mother gave him to a stranger to save his life, and he experienced it as a deep abandonment.

The story did not disappear. The facts stayed the same. What changed was the meaning, and with it the weight.

One of our own guests, Jay Poindexter, wrote in his [public review on BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/r/7-day-ayahuasca-retreat-at-an-authentic-finca-in-mexico-69537) that the ceremonies "enhanced my sense of self, expanded my awareness and acceptance of my dark and light sides." Acceptance, not a new story. That is often where it starts.

## Forgiveness and self-love

Lafrance separates two kinds of forgiveness. Decisional forgiveness is a choice to respond to someone in peace. Emotional forgiveness is when anger and grief are replaced by compassion. "Emotional forgiveness involves emotional transformation," she writes.

Her research team interviewed people about their ceremonial ayahuasca use. A number of them described emotional forgiveness toward parents, siblings and former partners, and toward themselves. She is honest that "the 'how' of this process remains unclear," and that ayahuasca is not the only way to forgive.

Self-love often arrives the same way, through the body before the mind. Another guest, who signed as Anonymous Yogi, wrote: "I had cathartic reactions that opened up my desires to be less judgmental, and more open to love for myself and others."

Maté put his own version simply: "I got in touch with such profound love that I had never consciously experienced before." In a [2017 interview with The Georgia Straight](https://www.straight.com/node/918656) he called it "a deep love that I both wanted and had been running away from all my life."

## Connection: to people, nature and something larger

Many people arrive feeling alone with their pain. Something in a ceremony circle tends to soften that.

Our guest Hiromi A wrote that "the deep connection I felt with the other guests were a gift." The study above found the same thing in its own language: better relationships with other people.

The connection often reaches further. People speak of feeling part of nature again, or of something larger than themselves, whatever name they give it. We wrote more about that side in [ayahuasca and spirituality](../blog/ayahuasca-and-spirituality).

Connection also means responsibility. Narby asks people who drink ayahuasca to remember that the brew comes from Amazonian peoples who are fighting for their land and their survival. He calls reciprocity "an antidote" to taking without giving, and suggests a simple practice: each time you drink, give what you can to an organization that supports indigenous Amazonian initiatives.

## Humility: the questions you did not know to ask

Narby says ayahuasca "has a knack for suggesting solutions to those who know how to ask pertinent questions." Many people find that the first lesson is that they were asking the wrong one.

You may come with the intention to understand your depression, your marriage or your drinking. The night may answer something else: grief you never mourned, or a fear you did not know was running your life. Our guest Sanjay put it in two lines: "Trust the process. The medicine has a way of finding what you need."

At MexiSol World we say there are no good or bad experiences, only experiences. We wrote about this in [what a hard night can teach](../blog/difficult-ayahuasca-ceremony). Some nights are full of images. Some are a long purge. Some feel like nothing happened, and the meaning arrives a week later. Humility is accepting the lesson you are given instead of the one you ordered.

That is also why intention matters. On the second day of our ayahuasca week, everyone sets intentions with Humberto before the first ceremony. An intention is a direction, not a demand. If you would like to know how the night itself unfolds, read [what to expect in your first ayahuasca ceremony](../blog/first-ayahuasca-ceremony).

## A lesson is only real when you live it

Here is the honest part. Maté, after that night of love, said: "I wish I could say that, after that experience, I became a loving husband and a loving human being. I didn't." But, he added, "it opened a door for me."

He also named what was missing: "I had no induction, I had no introduction, I had no processing afterwards." Today he insists that this work "has to be integrated" and is "not to be isolated from other kinds of work."

An open door is not the same as walking through it. Some ways people turn a lesson into a habit:

1. **Write it down in one sentence.** "I am not unlovable." "I can say no." Keep it where you will see it.
2. **Choose one practice for thirty days.** A daily call to someone you love, ten minutes of silence, a walk without your phone.
3. **Listen to your body.** Our guest who signed as Anonymous Yogi wrote: "I now pay more attention to listening to what my body is telling me, and I became willing to journal my feelings."
4. **Act on the forgiveness, gently.** A letter you may never send is still a step.
5. **Find people who can hold it.** A therapist, an integration circle or a friend who will not rush you.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does ayahuasca teach you, in simple words?**
Most people describe lessons about themselves: seeing old stories for what they are, forgiveness, self-acceptance, connection and humility. In a 2015 survey of 77 travellers, the most common benefits were self-knowledge and a change in how people relate to themselves. Every ceremony is different, and the lesson is rarely the one you expected.

**Why is ayahuasca called a teacher plant?**
In the Peruvian Amazon, vegetalista healers call certain plants "doctores" or plant teachers. They believe that, with isolation and a strict diet, these plants teach them how to heal. Anthropologists such as Luis Eduardo Luna and Jeremy Narby have documented this view for decades.

**Can ayahuasca give me back a sense of meaning?**
Many people say it did, often by changing how they see their past or their relationships. We cannot promise it, and we never present it as a treatment. What we can offer is a safe, held space, screening before you come and integration support after.

**What if I don't remember the lesson or nothing seems to happen?**
That is common. Quiet nights and confusing nights are part of the process too, and meaning often arrives days or weeks later. Writing down what you felt, even if it makes no sense yet, helps it surface.

**Do the lessons last?**
Only if you live them. Gabor Maté said his first ceremony "opened a door" but did not change him overnight. Integration, practice and support are what turn an insight into a new habit.
