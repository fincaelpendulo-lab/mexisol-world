The María Sabina mushroom ceremony is the Mazatec *velada*: a night vigil in the mountains of Oaxaca where a person with a real need, a wise woman or man who guides, and the sacred mushrooms, the *niños santos*, meet in the dark to ask for healing or answers. María Sabina, a healer from Huautla de Jiménez, made it known to the world in 1955, and her town paid a price for that fame.

Here is who she was, what the tradition asks of anyone who comes to it, what changed after the world found out, and what we take from it at MexiSol World, with respect and without claiming to be something we are not.

## Who María Sabina was

María Sabina was a *chjon chjine*, a wise woman, from Huautla de Jiménez in the Sierra Mazateca of Oaxaca. She spoke Mazatec, not Spanish. We know her life mostly through her own words, recorded by Álvaro Estrada and published in 1977 as *Vida de María Sabina. La sabia de los hongos* ([Siglo XXI](https://www.caniem.org/catalogo-venta-derechos/en/product/vida-de-maria-sabina-la-sabia-de-los-hongos/)).

As a [Mexican columnist at Vocero](https://www.vocero.com.mx/?p=371951) notes, Estrada stays almost hidden in the book and lets her speak. The tapes of her answers were kept.

As a girl she watched a healer treat her uncle: he handed out the mushrooms in pairs, called on the guardian spirits of the hills and springs, and chanted in the dark. Later she used them herself to heal her sick sister, as [this Chacruna essay](https://chacruna.net/maria-sabina-mushrooms-and-colonial-extractivism/) recounts. She came to see the work as something given to her, not something she chose.

## What a Mazatec velada is, and why it happens at night

The word comes from a Mazatec expression usually translated as "staying awake together". A [2024 paper in the journal Andamios](https://doi.org/10.29092/uacm.v21i54.1063) describes the velada as a Mazatec ceremony of divination and healing with the so-called *niños santos*, the psilocybin mushrooms.

In a [2020 panel with Inti García Flores, who is Mazatec, and two researchers](https://chacruna.net/mazatec_mushroom_ceremony_psychedelic_tourism/), the speakers explained that the velada always begins at nightfall, indoors, in the dark. It is private, not a group event.

What happens inside has a shape. The Andamios paper describes it this way:

- **The mushrooms are eaten in pairs.** Each pair is a duality: male and female, Jesus and the Virgin.
- **There is an altar** with saints and virgins, and the table faces east.
- **The healer censes the mushrooms with copal,** prays and sings to call in good forces and keep harm away.
- **The person who asked for the velada speaks to the mushroom.** They ask it to show them what they need to know.

The darkness is not decoration. In the tradition, it is where the mushroom speaks and teaches.

## The niños santos are not taken out of curiosity

In Huautla, nobody comes to a velada just to see what it feels like. People come with an illness, a problem, a loss. Ethnobotanist Mark Plotkin told the [Tim Ferriss Show](https://tim.blog/2022/07/04/mark-plotkin-and-hamilton-morris-transcript/) what a Oaxacan healer, Doña Julieta, said to him: before outsiders arrived, "we used the Niños Santos, the little sainted ones, the mushrooms, to heal and to search for lost objects."

### The diet before and after

The tradition asks for preparation. According to Andamios, the body must be clean to receive the mushroom, and this is called *la dieta*:

- No black beans and no pork.
- No sex.
- No accepting or sharing food and drink from others.

The Chacruna panel speaks of at least three days of sexual abstinence and stepping back from social life beforehand, as a time of introspection. The diet continues afterward, for at least four days, because, as one healer quoted in Andamios puts it, the mushroom says: "I am inside your body for four days."

### Who guides

Not just anyone guides, either. A [2019 study in the anthropology journal Cuicuilco](https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/cuicuilco/article/view/14716) explains that many healers were themselves cured of a serious illness and feel a duty to heal others. A man from Huautla told Mazatec researcher [Osiris García Cerqueda](https://chacruna.net/mushrooms-resistance-sierra-mazateca/) that this knowledge is learned slowly from parents and grandparents, and kept close "so that it doesn't get lost, so that it continues to heal people."

## Wasson, Life magazine and what changed in Huautla

R. Gordon Wasson was an American banker tied to J.P. Morgan. On the night of 29 June 1955, he and Allan Richardson took part in a velada guided by María Sabina, according to the [MAPS Psychedelic Bibliography record](https://bibliography.maps.org/bibliography/default/resource/15048). She agreed because Cayetano García, the municipal trustee, asked her to, and she felt torn about giving the mushrooms to someone no one knew.

On 13 May 1957, Wasson published his account in *Life* as "Seeking the Magic Mushroom". He did not use her real name: he called her Eva Méndez. Michael Pollan, author of [*How to Change Your Mind* (2018)](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/529343/how-to-change-your-mind-by-michael-pollan/), told [Tim Ferriss](https://tim.blog/2018/06/26/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-michael-pollan/) that Time-Life's owner, Henry Luce, actively promoted psychedelics until 1965.

### What came next

Thousands came. Anthropologist Benjamin Feinberg, in [*The Devil's Book of Culture* (University of Texas Press, 2003)](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292701908/the-devils-book-of-culture/), describes "a strange assortment of visitors and pilgrims" arriving from the 1950s on: hippies, cave explorers, New Age seekers.

For María Sabina it was a disaster. Part of her community blamed her for revealing something sacred. Unknown people burned her house, one of her sons was killed, and she died in poverty in 1985. In a 1976 interview with Estrada she said: "It is true that before Wasson, no one spoke so freely about children," meaning the mushrooms, and "But I obeyed the municipal trustee." Her great-grandson, the healer Anselmo García Martínez, put it plainly to [Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/culture/magic-mushrooms-mexicos-shamans/): "They did not respect the mushroom."

### Huautla today

Huautla became a Pueblo Mágico in 2015. The Cuicuilco study documents veladas for tourists sold in cabins, online or at the bus station, and locals who call them charlatanism because they skip the diagnosis a true healer makes. García Cerqueda writes of "a sacred tourism industry" that turns Mazatec culture into money. The Chacruna panel adds mushroom shortages in recent years, linked to changes in land use and to outside demand.

## What the María Sabina mushroom ceremony teaches us today

We are not Mazatec and we do not carry their lineage. We say this clearly, because one of the community's own complaints is outsiders selling "veladas" in their name. What we can do is listen to what the tradition teaches and apply it honestly.

Krista, who guides our mushroom ceremonies, studied in the Mazatec mountains of Oaxaca directly with descendants of María Sabina. There she learned how to share this tradition with respect, care and honor for where it comes from. That learning shapes how she holds every ceremony with us.

### Come with a question, not curiosity

A velada begins with a need. So before any ceremony, we ask you for an intention: what brings you, what you want to look at, what pattern or habit you are ready to let go of. It does not have to be perfect. It has to be yours.

### Preparation is part of the ceremony

The Mazatec diet is not only about food. It is stepping back, cleaning up, arriving with your body and attention available. The Andamios paper argues that these beliefs, the active role of the person seeking, the figure of the guide and the space itself all shape how psilocybin acts. It is a theoretical paper, not a clinical trial, but it matches what guests tell us: arriving prepared changes the experience.

### What happens after matters as much as the night

The four days of care after a velada have a modern echo: integration. What you saw in ceremony becomes real when you carry it home, into your habits and your relationships. For us there are no good or bad experiences, only experiences, and the hard ones bring something to work with too.

## Frequently asked questions

**What are the niños santos?**
It is the affectionate Mazatec name for psilocybin mushrooms, the "holy children". They are also called the Honguito or "the little ones that sprout". In the tradition they have their own personality: they are the ones who speak and teach during the velada.

**What happened to María Sabina after Wasson?**
Her fame brought thousands of visitors to Huautla. Part of her community blamed her, her house was burned, and she died in poverty in 1985. Today her image is used to sell tourist experiences in the same town.

**Is a retreat ceremony the same as a Mazatec velada?**
No. A velada is a private Mazatec practice, guided by a healer from that tradition. Our retreats are something else: a week with preparation, several ceremonies, support and integration. We take what the tradition teaches about intention, preparation and respect, without claiming to be part of it.

**How long does a velada last?**
It begins at nightfall and, as the name says, it is a night spent awake. Psilocybin taken by mouth usually lasts about six hours, as we explain in our [guide to psilocybin retreats in Mexico](../blog/psilocybin-retreat-mexico).
