How to stay sober during the holidays comes down to three things: know what the craving is really asking for, decide before each gathering who you want to be there, and give your hands and heart something else to hold. Plan your hardest moments in advance, keep your people close, and if you slip, begin again the next morning.

The tips on most lists are useful: bring your own drink, have an exit plan, call someone. This article adds the inner side. December is a month of rituals, and many of them run on alcohol. You can keep the ritual and change what is in the cup.

If you drink every day or heavily, read the safety note further down before you stop. Stopping suddenly can be dangerous, and that part needs a doctor.

## Why December is the hardest month for a habit

Relapse researchers have a name for the moments when an old habit is most likely to win: high-risk situations. In a [review of Alan Marlatt's relapse prevention model](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10890810/), published by the US National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, the list reads like a December calendar.

Negative emotions such as anger, loneliness, frustration and boredom were "associated with the highest rate of relapse." Conflict with other people came next, with "an argument with a family member" as the example. Together they were behind more than half of relapse episodes in Marlatt's analysis.

Social pressure, including just being around people who drink, played a part in more than 20 percent. The same review also lists celebrations, cues like a favorite bar, and testing your own willpower. December brings all of them at once, often in the same room.

There is a cultural layer too. "It's ingrained in us that celebrating equals alcohol," Racheal Williams, three years sober, told [VICE](https://www.vice.com/en/article/why-im-spending-this-christmas-sober). In Mexico it is the posada, the Christmas Eve dinner and the toast at midnight. Almost every family gathering has a glass in it.

So if this month feels harder, it is not because you are weak. It is because the season is built from your triggers.

## What the craving is really asking for

Psychiatrist Judson Brewer, who studies mindfulness and addiction, describes the loop in his [TEDMED talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/judson_brewer_a_simple_way_to_break_a_bad_habit) in three words: "Trigger, behavior, reward." In his book [*The Craving Mind*](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/?p=8974) he explains why a party can set it off before you even see a drink: the cues are ones "we have learned to pair with rewarding behavior."

His answer is not more willpower. It is curiosity. He asks people to get "really curious about what was happening in our momentary experience." When they look closely, he says, "we start to notice that cravings are simply made up of body sensations": tightness, heat, restlessness. Sensations rise, peak and pass.

Research on "urge surfing" points the same way. In a [2009 randomized study](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017127) of 123 students who smoked, brief mindfulness instructions did not make the urges any weaker. But the students who learned them smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over the next seven days. The authors concluded that mindfulness "may change the response to urges." It was a small study, about tobacco, with a short follow-up. The idea still helps: you do not have to win against the feeling, only let it pass without obeying it.

### The thirst under the thirst

Then there is the deeper question. In a [1961 letter to Bill Wilson](https://wccm.org/outreach-areas/addiction-recovery/carl-jung-letter-to-bill-w), the co-founder of Alcoholics Anonymous, Carl Jung described a patient's craving for alcohol as a low form of "the spiritual thirst of our being for wholeness." His formula was "spiritus contra spiritum": spirit against spirit.

At a December table, that thirst often has a simpler name. Belonging. Rest. Being seen by your family as you are now, not as you were at 17. Relief from a year that was too much.

Next time the craving comes, try asking it, quietly: what do you want for me right now? The answer is rarely "a drink." It is usually something you can give yourself another way.

## How to stay sober during the holidays: set an intention first

An intention is not a rule. A rule says "I will not drink." An intention says who you want to be in that room: present with my kids, honest with my brother, home by eleven and clear in the morning.

Write it down before you leave, in one line. Say it to yourself in the car. It turns a night you are afraid of into a night with a purpose.

Clinicians who work with addiction recommend the practical side of this too. In an [EFE report on Christmas and alcohol](https://www.eldiario.es/sociedad/navidad-personas-alcoholismo-anticipacion-psicologica-apoyo-social_1_12870226.html), Gerard Mateu Codina of Hospital del Mar in Barcelona describes planning ahead what a person will do and which events they will go to. Not every invitation needs a yes.

### Make an if-then plan

Psychologists call it an implementation intention: "If my uncle pours me a drink, then I say 'I'm good, thanks' and go fill my glass with sparkling water." A [2022 meta-analysis of 16 studies](https://doi.org/10.1111/dar.13553) found that if-then plans reduced weekly drinking, but the effect was small and faded over time, and it did not reduce heavy drinking episodes.

So treat them as one tool among several. Write two or three for the situations you know are coming:

- **If someone offers a drink,** I say no thanks and hold my own glass.
- **If I feel the old restlessness after dinner,** I step outside and breathe for five minutes.
- **If an argument starts,** I go help in the kitchen or leave early.

Duncan Hutchinson, quoted in the same VICE piece, adds two simple ones: tell the people you will see "ahead of time" that you are not drinking, and bring your own alcohol-free drinks.

## Small rituals that replace the drink

A drink at a party is not only alcohol. It is something in your hand, a reason to pause, a way to join the toast. If you take it away and leave nothing, the gap pulls hard.

Annie Grace writes in [*This Naked Mind*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/577190/this-naked-mind-by-annie-grace/) that most people "assume giving it up will involve deprivation and misery." Rituals are how you prove the opposite to yourself.

Some that people use:

1. **A drink of your own.** Hibiscus water, ponche without the piquete, a good tea, sparkling juice in a real glass. Sam Thomas, a few weeks sober, spent Christmas with "fizzy grape juice" and called it the best one of their life in a [first-person essay for HuffPost UK](https://www.huffingtonpost.co.uk/entry/i-spent-my-first-sober-christmas-alone-it-was-the-best-one-i-ever-had_uk_6582b664e4b0d9bdbf682b64).
2. **A candle before you go.** One minute of silence and your intention spoken out loud.
3. **A toast with your own words.** You can still raise a glass. What you say matters more than what is in it.
4. **A walk.** Before dinner, after dinner, or both. Movement lets the sensations pass.
5. **A morning page.** Write three lines the day after: what was hard, what you did, what you are grateful for.

That last one is how small changes start. One of our guests, Anonymous Yogi, wrote in a [review on BookRetreats](https://bookretreats.com/r/7-day-ayahuasca-retreat-at-an-authentic-finca-in-mexico-69537): "I now pay more attention to listening to what my body is telling me, and I became willing to journal my feelings."

## Family triggers during the holidays: old roles, old wounds

Ram Dass put it with a smile. On the site for his memoir [*Being Ram Dass*](https://beingramdass.soundstrue.com) there is this line: "If you want to see how you are doing on your spiritual path, go spend a week with your parents."

Families pull us back into old roles. The one who drinks, the black sheep, the one who has to be fine. When you change, people around you may not know where to put you yet. Some will test it, without meaning harm.

A few things help:

- **You don't owe an explanation.** "I'm not drinking tonight" is a full sentence.
- **Choose your allies.** Tell one person at the gathering what you are doing and ask them to back you.
- **Shorten the time.** Arrive late, leave early, or stay for the meal and skip the after-party.
- **See the role, don't play it.** When you feel yourself becoming 17 again, notice it. That noticing is already a step out.

Families who want to help can do a lot. In the EFE report, Rebeca Hernández Medina of Projecte Home Catalunya says families and the people who support someone in treatment play "un papel esencial", an essential role. If someone in your family is the one changing, the kindest gift may be a table without bottles on it.

## If you slip: begin again tomorrow

A slip in December does not erase what you have built. How you treat yourself the morning after matters more than the slip itself.

Marlatt's model, in the [same review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10890810/), calls one trap the abstinence violation effect. People who blame a lapse on personal failure feel guilt, and that guilt can lead to more drinking to escape it. The thought sounds like "I have no willpower and will never be able to stop." The review is clear that "the progression from lapse to relapse is not inevitable."

So treat a slip as information. What was the situation? What were you feeling just before? What would you do differently? Then call someone, drink water, sleep, and start again.

Annie Grace said it plainly in a [2021 interview with RNZ](https://www.rnz.co.nz/national/programmes/summer-days/audio/2018779837/giving-up-alcohol-with-awareness-not-just-abstinence): "I really believe that we can be empowered to change." Shame keeps the loop running. Compassion lets you leave it.

### A safety note we will not skip

If you drink heavily or every day, do not stop suddenly on your own. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000764.htm), from the US National Library of Medicine, warns that "alcohol withdrawal is a serious condition that may quickly become life-threatening," with symptoms usually starting within 8 hours of the last drink. Talk to a doctor first, and seek urgent care if you have seizures, confusion or hallucinations.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I stay sober at Christmas dinner with family?**
Decide your intention before you arrive and bring your own drink. Tell one ally what you are doing, and have an exit plan if tension rises. You don't have to explain yourself: "I'm not drinking tonight" is enough.

**How do I get through a sober New Year's Eve?**
Choose where you will be and with whom, instead of going wherever the party is. Make the midnight toast your own, with a drink you like and words that mean something. Many people prefer a quiet ritual or an early night and a clear first morning of the year.

**What should I do if I drink during the holidays after deciding not to?**
Be honest and kind with yourself. A lapse is not a relapse unless you let guilt turn it into one. Look at what triggered it, talk to someone you trust, and begin again the next day.

**Can I stop drinking right before a retreat?**
If you drink heavily or daily, stopping suddenly can be dangerous, so talk to a doctor first. Guests must arrive free of alcohol, and active alcohol dependence may mean a retreat is not safe for you yet. That is why we talk with every guest before confirming anything.
