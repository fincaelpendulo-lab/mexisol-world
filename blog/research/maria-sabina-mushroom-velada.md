# Research: maria-sabina-mushroom-velada

Keyword: "María Sabina mushroom ceremony" / "qué es una velada de hongos". Profile: Local Seeker. Publish date 2026-11-14.
Spanish-first post. Angle: tell the story (Huautla, veladas, Wasson 1955, Life 1957, tourism), what the Mazatec tradition
asks (preparation, need, not curiosity), and what we take from it, without claiming any Mazatec lineage.

## Sources

### Books

1. **Vida de María Sabina. La sabia de los hongos.** Álvaro Estrada. Siglo XXI Editores. First published 1977
   (year confirmed by the Vocero column, source 12); current edition 2022, ISBN 9786070312380.
   https://www.caniem.org/catalogo-venta-derechos/en/product/vida-de-maria-sabina-la-sabia-de-los-hongos/ Type: book (oral autobiography).
   Publisher rights catalogue (CANIEM): born in the Mazatec mountains, she "turned sacred mushrooms into a bridge between the
   earthly and the divine"; her chants were recorded by Estrada; her worldview merged nature and spirituality "with poverty
   and loss"; "Without seeking fame, she transformed culture and left a universal legacy."

2. **How to Change Your Mind.** Michael Pollan. Penguin Press / Penguin Books, 2018.
   https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/529343/how-to-change-your-mind-by-michael-pollan/ Type: book.
   Publisher page: "A brilliant and brave investigation into the medical and scientific revolution taking place around
   psychedelic drugs." Used only to introduce Pollan; what we cite from him comes from his Tim Ferriss interview (source 5).

3. **The Devil's Book of Culture: History, Mushrooms, and Caves in Southern Mexico.** Benjamin Feinberg. University of
   Texas Press, 2003. https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292701908/the-devils-book-of-culture/ Type: book (ethnography).
   Since the 1950s the Sierra Mazateca has drawn "a strange assortment of visitors and pilgrims": teachers, government
   workers, cavers, hippies, New Age seekers, who come to take mushrooms "in context" with a Mazatec shaman. Argues
   Mazatec identity is not a static thing later changed by contact; it forms at the intersection of many discourses.

### Talks, interviews, podcasts (with transcripts)

4. **Niños Santos, Psilocybin Mushrooms and the Psychedelic Renaissance.** Inti García Flores, Rosalía Acosta López, Saraí
   Piña Alcántara. Chacruna, 12 Nov 2020. Transcript of their panel at Tam Integration's Psilocybin Summit 2020.
   https://chacruna.net/mazatec_mushroom_ceremony_psychedelic_tourism/ Type: talk (panel transcript).
   Credibility: Inti García Flores speaks as a Mazatec; Rosalía Acosta is a psychologist who has lived in Huautla ~20 years;
   Saraí Piña is the CIESAS anthropologist of source 9.
   Velada: night-time ceremony with the ndi xijtho ("little ones that sprout"), indoors, in the dark, "always begins at
   nightfall"; private, not a group ceremony. At least three days of sexual abstinence and social withdrawal beforehand.
   Purpose: heal illness, recover a lost "shadow". After: recommendations, continued restrictions. Visitors asked to come with
   respect, not expect paid access or demand adaptation. Tourism: ceremonies sold by neoshamans; mushroom shortages linked to
   land use and foreign demand; some facilitators accused of taking Mazatec techniques outside the region.

5. **The Tim Ferriss Show #313: Michael Pollan.** Transcript, tim.blog, 26 June 2018.
   https://tim.blog/2018/06/26/the-tim-ferriss-show-transcripts-michael-pollan/ Type: podcast interview.
   Pollan: Time-Life, controlled by Henry Luce, actively promoted psychedelics until 1965; Luce "published Gordon Wasson's
   first account of a magic mushroom experience in Mexico in the 1950s." Pollan calls María Sabina "this legendary figure in
   psychedelic history who gave Gordon Wasson ... his ceremony in Oaxaca back in the '50s."

6. **The Tim Ferriss Show #605: Hamilton Morris and Dr. Mark Plotkin.** Transcript, tim.blog, 4 July 2022.
   https://tim.blog/2022/07/04/mark-plotkin-and-hamilton-morris-transcript/ Type: podcast interview.
   Credibility: Plotkin is an ethnobotanist, president of the Amazon Conservation Team. Plotkin worked ~20 years earlier in
   Oaxaca with Doña Julieta, who told him: "Before the Gringo started showing up here, before people like Wasson showed up
   here, we used the Niños Santos, the little sainted ones, the mushrooms, to heal and to search for lost objects." In
   Huautla in the early 2000s he saw people in the plaza holding Plants of the Gods "going like, 'I think I'll try that one'".

### Testimonials (first-person, recorded words)

7. **María Sabina, 1976 interview with Álvaro Estrada**, as quoted in: Osiris Sinuhé González Romero, "María Sabina,
   Mushrooms, and Colonial Extractivism." Chacruna, 27 May 2021.
   https://chacruna.net/maria-sabina-mushrooms-and-colonial-extractivism/ Type: testimonial quoted in a credible outlet.
   Her words (1976, to Estrada): "It is true that before Wasson, no one spoke so freely about children"; "None of our people
   revealed what they knew about this matter"; "But I obeyed the municipal trustee." Article also: as a child she watched a
   sage heal her uncle, giving mushrooms in pairs and chanting in the dark; she later healed her sister; Wasson was a banker
   tied to J.P. Morgan; the municipal trustee pressed her; afterward her house was burned, a son was murdered, she died in
   poverty in 1985. Also quotes "I should have said no" (no source named in the article: not used).

8. **Anselmo García Martínez (María Sabina's great-grandson, curandero)**, quoted in Joseph Sorrentino, "Mexico's
   graniceros use magic mushrooms to speak to the divine." Mexico News Daily, 9 May 2023.
   https://mexiconewsdaily.com/culture/magic-mushrooms-mexicos-shamans/ Type: media + testimonial.
   1955: the Wassons persuaded her after repeated attempts; publicity brought thousands of outsiders, a disaster for her and
   her community; residents turned against her; she died in poverty. Great-grandson: "They did not respect the mushroom."
   Article: "Too many people were going — and are still going — to Huautla just to get high."

### Research and academic

9. **Turismo y chamanismo, dos mundos imbricados: el caso de Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca.** Saraí Piña Alcántara (CIESAS).
   Cuicuilco Revista de Ciencias Antropológicas 26(75):43-66, 2019 (INAH/ENAH).
   https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/cuicuilco/article/view/14716 (full text read on Redalyc). Type: anthropological study.
   Chjota chjine are often people cured of a serious illness who then have a duty to heal; work includes "velar con los
   hongos". Tourist veladas offered in cabins, online or at the bus terminal; locals often do not recognize these providers
   and some call them charlatans because they skip the diagnostic process a chjota chjine performs. Tourists also take
   mushrooms alone in rented cabins making their own rituals. Promotion grew after Pueblo Mágico status in 2015. Calls
   María Sabina "la primera neochamana autóctona"; her image is used to market velada experiences.

10. **Cognición extendida y símbolos de la velada mazateca con hongos psilocibios.** Seriad Caynas Rojas, Roberto E.
    Mercadillo. Andamios (UACM) 21(54):179-, 2024. https://doi.org/10.29092/uacm.v21i54.1063 Type: theoretical review
    built on recent ethnographies (Fagetti & Mercadillo 2022, others).
    María Sabina, chjo̱ on chji̱ ne̱ (mujer sabia), guided Wasson in a velada (Mazatec tsakjena kón ka'oña, "desvelarse
    juntos"), a Mazatec ceremony of divination and healing with the "Niños Santos"; in 1957 Wasson published in Life, which
    revealed the velada "a la curiosidad e interés del mundo occidental". Participation requires days of preparation;
    dieta (nixin tjinlee): no black beans or pork, no sex, not receiving or sharing food; kept at least four days after
    ("Estoy dentro de tu cuerpo cuatro días"). Mushrooms eaten in pairs (male/female, Jesús and the Virgen). The person asks
    the Honguito to reveal what they want to know, "que lo ilumine"; the patient plays an active role. Healer censes
    mushrooms with copal, prays and sings. Conclusion: beliefs about sacredness, the active role of the patient, the healer
    and the space are symbolic elements that shape the effects; the velada offers guidance for psychedelic medicine today.
    Theoretical paper, not a clinical trial.

11. **Seeking the Magic Mushroom.** R. Gordon Wasson. Life 42(19), 13 May 1957, pp. 100-120. MAPS Psychedelic
    Bibliography record with full text: https://bibliography.maps.org/bibliography/default/resource/15048 Type: primary
    historical source. MAPS abstract: describes Wasson and Allan Richardson's participation "on the night of 29 June 1955" in
    a mushroom ceremony guided by curandera "Eva Mendez [real name Maria Sabina]"; the article helped seed the 1960s
    cultural revolution. (MAPS record lists 1959 in its date field, but the file name and Life's Google Books issue,
    https://books.google.com/books?id=Jj8EAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA100, show 13 May 1957; Andamios also gives 1957.)

### Media and creators

12. **OTRAS INQUISICIONES: En busca del hongo mágico.** Pablo Cabañas Díaz. Vocero, 25 Jan 2024.
    https://www.vocero.com.mx/?p=371951 Type: Mexican opinion column.
    Estrada published Vida de María Sabina in 1977; he largely keeps himself out of the narration and gives her the voice;
    the tapes of her answers were kept; Estrada acknowledged the difficulty of writing about someone who did not read, write
    or speak Spanish.

13. **Magic Mushrooms, Memory and Resistance in the Sierra Mazateca.** Osiris García Cerqueda, Ph.D. (Mazatec researcher).
    Chacruna, 18 Aug 2020. https://chacruna.net/mushrooms-resistance-sierra-mazateca/ Type: Mazatec scholar's essay.
    María Sabina agreed to the June 1955 velada after Cayetano García asked her; she felt a moral dilemma about giving mushrooms
    to someone unknown to the community. She felt the sacred world assigned her the work so the mushrooms "could speak and heal
    people". Tourism created "a sacred tourism industry that strengthens and reproduces the monetization of Mazatec culture."
    Local man Serapio: healers learn gradually from parents and grandparents and keep it secret "so that it doesn't get lost,
    so that it continues to heal people."

## Claims in the post and their sources

- María Sabina was a Mazatec chjon chjine / "mujer sabia" in Huautla de Jiménez, Oaxaca: 10, 13.
- As a child she saw a healer treat her uncle with mushrooms in pairs, chanting in the dark; later healed her sister: 7.
- She spoke Mazatec, not Spanish; Estrada recorded her answers and published her oral life story in 1977 (Siglo XXI): 1, 12.
- "Velada" comes from the Mazatec idea of staying awake together; a ceremony of divination and healing with the niños santos: 10.
- The velada begins at nightfall, indoors, in the dark, private, not a group event: 4.
- Mushrooms eaten in pairs; copal, prayer, song, altar: 10 (and 7 for pairs).
- Preparation: dieta with no black beans or pork, no sex, not sharing food; at least 3 days abstinence and withdrawal before;
  care for at least four days after: 4, 10.
- The person comes with a problem and asks to be shown/illuminated; active role: 10. Healing and finding lost objects: 6.
- Healers often were cured of a serious illness themselves and learned gradually from family: 9, 13.
- June 1955 (night of 29 June) Wasson and Allan Richardson took part in a velada with her; Cayetano García (municipal
  trustee) asked her: 11, 13, 7.
- Life, 13 May 1957, "Seeking the Magic Mushroom", called her "Eva Méndez": 11. Luce/Time-Life published it: 5.
- Her 1976 words to Estrada: 7.
- Aftermath: thousands of outsiders, hippies and New Age seekers since the 1950s/60s; her house burned, a son killed, died in
  poverty in 1985: 3, 7, 8. Great-grandson's words: 8.
- Plotkin's early 2000s plaza scene: 6.
- Today: Pueblo Mágico 2015; veladas sold in cabins and at the bus terminal; locals call some charlatans: 9.
  Sacred tourism industry, monetization: 13. Mushroom shortages, neoshamans, requests for respect: 4.
- Research: meaning, preparation, active role of the person and setting shape the experience (theoretical, 2024): 10.
